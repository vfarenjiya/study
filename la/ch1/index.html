1. Vector Spaces and Subspaces

Chapter 1 of 14 16 min read

Why we need axioms instead of intuition

In calculus and early linear algebra courses, vectors are often introduced as arrows in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) or \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and operations are defined geometrically. This intuition is valuable but insufficient for advanced work: polynomials, matrices, signals, and solution sets of differential equations all behave like vectors, yet none are arrows in space. To unify these objects under a single theory, we must abandon geometry and define a vector space purely by the algebraic rules its operations obey. This abstraction is not pedantry—it is what allows theorems proved once for abstract vector spaces to apply immediately to every concrete instance, from quantum states to error-correcting codes.

The payoff for this abstraction appears throughout this book: the rank-nullity theorem (Chapter 5), eigenvalue theory (Chapter 7), and singular value decomposition (Chapter 13) are all statements about abstract vector spaces and linear maps between them. If you internalize only the \(\mathbb{R}^n\) picture, you will struggle when these theorems are applied to function spaces or matrix spaces. The axioms below are the minimal contract that makes all later results valid.

The ten vector space axioms

A vector space over a field \(F\) (typically \(F = \mathbb{R}\) or \(F = \mathbb{C}\)) is a set \(V\) equipped with two operations—addition \(+: V \times V \to V\) and scalar multiplication \(\cdot: F \times V \to V\)—satisfying the following ten axioms for all \(u, v, w \in V\) and all \(a, b \in F\):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{(A1)} && u + v \in V \\ &\text{(A2)} && u + v = v + u \\ &\text{(A3)} && (u + v) + w = u + (v + w) \\ &\text{(A4)} && \exists\, \mathbf{0} \in V \text{ such that } v + \mathbf{0} = v \;\forall v \\ &\text{(A5)} && \forall v \in V,\; \exists\, (-v) \in V \text{ such that } v + (-v) = \mathbf{0} \\ &\text{(M1)} && a v \in V \\ &\text{(M2)} && a(u + v) = au + av \\ &\text{(M3)} && (a + b)v = av + bv \\ &\text{(M4)} && a(bv) = (ab)v \\ &\text{(M5)} && 1 \cdot v = v \end{aligned} \]

Axioms A1 and M1 are closure properties: they guarantee that the operations do not produce objects outside \(V\). Without closure, the remaining axioms would refer to undefined expressions. Axioms A2–A5 make \((V, +)\) an abelian group. Axioms M2–M5 encode how scalars interact with vectors; notably, M5 ties the multiplicative identity of the field to the identity action on vectors. Every object called a “vector space” in mathematics satisfies exactly these ten conditions—no more, no less.

Common mistake: Students frequently verify A2–A5 and M2–M5 but skip A1 and M1, assuming closure is “obvious.” It is not. The set of all polynomials of degree exactly 3 is closed under scalar multiplication but not under addition (the sum of two degree-3 polynomials can have lower degree), so it fails A1 and is not a vector space. Always check closure first.

Worked example: verifying \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a vector space

Let \(V = \mathbb{R}^3\) with componentwise addition and scalar multiplication. We verify three representative axioms explicitly; the others follow identically.

A1 (Closure under addition): Let \(u = (1, -2, 4)\) and \(v = (3, 0, -1)\). Then \(u + v = (1+3, -2+0, 4+(-1)) = (4, -2, 3)\). Each component is a real number, so \(u+v \in \mathbb{R}^3\). Since addition of reals is closed, this holds for all \(u, v \in \mathbb{R}^3\).

M3 (Distributivity over scalar addition): Let \(a = 2\), \(b = -1\), and \(v = (3, 0, -1)\). Left side: \((a+b)v = (2 + (-1))v = 1 \cdot v = (3, 0, -1)\). Right side: \(av + bv = 2(3,0,-1) + (-1)(3,0,-1) = (6,0,-2) + (-3,0,1) = (3,0,-1)\). Both sides match. The equality holds componentwise because real numbers satisfy distributivity.

A4 (Zero vector): The candidate is \(\mathbf{0} = (0,0,0)\). For any \(v = (v_1,v_2,v_3)\), \(v + \mathbf{0} = (v_1+0, v_2+0, v_3+0) = (v_1,v_2,v_3) = v\). Thus \(\mathbf{0}\) exists and satisfies the axiom.

This verification illustrates the general pattern: properties of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) reduce to properties of \(\mathbb{R}\) applied componentwise. The same logic extends to \(\mathbb{R}^n\) for any \(n\), and to \(\mathbb{C}^n\) with complex arithmetic.

Subspaces and the subspace test

A subspace of a vector space \(V\) is a subset \(W \subseteq V\) that is itself a vector space under the same operations inherited from \(V\). Checking all ten axioms for \(W\) is redundant: since \(W\) inherits associativity, commutativity, and distributivity from \(V\), only three conditions need verification. This yields the subspace test:

\[ W \subseteq V \text{ is a subspace} \iff \begin{cases} \mathbf{0} \in W, \\ \forall u,v \in W,\; u+v \in W, \\ \forall c \in F,\; \forall u \in W,\; cu \in W. \end{cases} \]

The zero-vector condition is essential and non-negotiable. If \(\mathbf{0} \notin W\), then \(W\) cannot be a vector space regardless of closure under addition and scalar multiplication. In practice, checking \(\mathbf{0} \in W\) is often the fastest way to disqualify a candidate subset. When \(W\) is defined as the solution set of a homogeneous equation \(Ax = \mathbf{0}\), the zero vector is automatically included because \(A\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}\); this is why homogeneous systems always define subspaces, while non-homogeneous systems \(Ax = b\) with \(b \neq \mathbf{0}\) never do.

Edge case: The trivial subspace \(\{\mathbf{0}\}\) satisfies the subspace test: it contains \(\mathbf{0}\), and \(\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}\), \(c\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}\). At the other extreme, \(V\) itself is always a subspace of \(V\). These are the only subspaces of a 1-dimensional vector space; higher-dimensional spaces admit infinitely many proper nontrivial subspaces.

Worked example: a plane through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Let \(W = \{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : 2x - y + 3z = 0\}\). We apply the subspace test step by step.

Step 1 (Zero vector): Substitute \(x=y=z=0\): \(2(0) - 0 + 3(0) = 0\). Satisfied.

Step 2 (Closure under addition): Let \(u = (x_1,y_1,z_1)\) and \(v = (x_2,y_2,z_2)\) be in \(W\), so \(2x_1 - y_1 + 3z_1 = 0\) and \(2x_2 - y_2 + 3z_2 = 0\). Compute \(u+v = (x_1+x_2, y_1+y_2, z_1+z_2)\). Check membership: \(2(x_1+x_2) - (y_1+y_2) + 3(z_1+z_2) = (2x_1 - y_1 + 3z_1) + (2x_2 - y_2 + 3z_2) = 0 + 0 = 0\). So \(u+v \in W\).

Step 3 (Closure under scalar multiplication): Let \(c \in \mathbb{R}\) and \(u = (x_1,y_1,z_1) \in W\). Then \(cu = (cx_1, cy_1, cz_1)\). Check: \(2(cx_1) - (cy_1) + 3(cz_1) = c(2x_1 - y_1 + 3z_1) = c \cdot 0 = 0\). So \(cu \in W\).

All three conditions hold; \(W\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Geometrically, it is a plane through the origin. Had the defining equation been \(2x - y + 3z = 5\), Step 1 would fail (\(0 \neq 5\)), and \(W\) would not be a subspace—a critical distinction for understanding solution sets in Chapter 4.

Span and linear combinations

Given vectors \(v_1, \dots, v_k \in V\), a linear combination is any expression \(c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_k v_k\) with \(c_i \in F\). The span of \(\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}\) is the set of all such combinations:

\[ \operatorname{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_k\} = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k} c_i v_i : c_i \in F \right\}. \]

The span is always a subspace of \(V\). This is one of the most important constructive facts in linear algebra: given any collection of vectors (even an infinite one, with finite linear combinations), their span is guaranteed to be a subspace. Conversely, every subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space equals the span of some finite set of vectors—this is the content of the basis existence theorem in Section 5.

Derivation: span is a subspace. Let \(S = \operatorname{span}\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}\). First, \(\mathbf{0} = 0v_1 + \cdots + 0v_k \in S\). Second, let \(u = \sum a_i v_i\) and \(w = \sum b_i v_i\) be in \(S\). Then \(u + w = \sum (a_i + b_i) v_i \in S\) because each \(a_i + b_i \in F\). Third, for \(c \in F\), \(cu = \sum (c a_i) v_i \in S\). Thus \(S\) passes the subspace test. Note that this proof uses only the vector space axioms and the definition of linear combination—it does not depend on what \(V\) concretely is.

Worked example: computing span via row reduction

Determine whether \(w = (5, 1, -1)\) lies in \(\operatorname{span}\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}\) where \(v_1 = (1,0,1)\), \(v_2 = (0,1,-1)\), \(v_3 = (1,1,0)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

We seek scalars \(c_1, c_2, c_3\) such that \(c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + c_3 v_3 = w\). This gives the system:

\[ \begin{cases} c_1 + c_3 = 5 \\ c_2 + c_3 = 1 \\ c_1 - c_2 = -1 \end{cases} \]

Form the augmented matrix and row-reduce:

\[ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 & -1 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_3 \gets R_3 - R_1} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -1 & -6 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_3 \gets R_3 + R_2} \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -5 \end{array}\right]. \]

The last row reads \(0 = -5\), which is inconsistent. Therefore no solution exists, and \(w \notin \operatorname{span}\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}\). Had the right-hand side been \((5,1,4)\) instead, the last entry would become \(0=0\), yielding infinitely many solutions (one free variable), confirming membership. This direct link between span membership and linear system consistency is foundational for Gaussian elimination in Chapter 4.

Basis, dimension, and coordinates

A set \(B = \{b_1, \dots, b_n\} \subseteq V\) is a basis of \(V\) if it is linearly independent (defined rigorously in Chapter 2) and \(\operatorname{span}(B) = V\). The dimension of \(V\), denoted \(\dim(V)\), is the cardinality of any basis. A fundamental theorem (proved in Chapter 2) guarantees that all bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same size, making dimension well-defined.

Once a basis \(B\) is fixed, every \(v \in V\) has a unique representation \(v = c_1 b_1 + \cdots + c_n b_n\). The tuple \([v]_B = (c_1, \dots, c_n)^\top \in F^n\) is the coordinate vector of \(v\) relative to \(B\). Coordinates convert abstract vector space problems into concrete \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (or \(F^n\)) computations. This isomorphism \(V \cong F^n\) is why we can use matrices to represent linear transformations between arbitrary finite-dimensional spaces—a bridge fully developed in Chapter 3 and exploited in LU decomposition (Chapter 12) and SVD (Chapter 13).

Common mistake: Dimension is a property of the space, not of individual vectors or spanning sets. Saying “these three vectors have dimension 3” is meaningless; the correct statement is “these three vectors form a basis for a 3-dimensional space.” Also, \(\dim(\{\mathbf{0}\}) = 0\) by convention—the empty set is the basis of the trivial subspace. Forgetting this causes errors in rank-nullity calculations (Chapter 5) when the null space is trivial.

Worked example: coordinates in a non-standard basis

Let \(V = P_2\), the space of polynomials of degree at most 2, with standard basis \(E = \{1, x, x^2\}\). Consider the alternative basis \(B = \{1, (x-1), (x-1)^2\}\). Find \([p]_B\) for \(p(x) = 3x^2 - 2x + 4\).

We seek \(c_0, c_1, c_2\) such that \(p(x) = c_0 \cdot 1 + c_1 (x-1) + c_2 (x-1)^2\). Expand the right side:

\[ c_0 + c_1(x-1) + c_2(x^2 - 2x + 1) = c_2 x^2 + (c_1 - 2c_2)x + (c_0 - c_1 + c_2). \]

Equate coefficients with \(p(x) = 3x^2 - 2x + 4\):

\[ \begin{cases} c_2 = 3 \\ c_1 - 2c_2 = -2 \\ c_0 - c_1 + c_2 = 4 \end{cases} \]

Solve bottom-up: \(c_2 = 3\). Then \(c_1 - 6 = -2 \Rightarrow c_1 = 4\). Then \(c_0 - 4 + 3 = 4 \Rightarrow c_0 = 5\). Thus \([p]_B = (5, 4, 3)^\top\). Verify: \(5 \cdot 1 + 4(x-1) + 3(x-1)^2 = 5 + 4x - 4 + 3x^2 - 6x + 3 = 3x^2 - 2x + 4\). Correct. This computation shows that changing basis is equivalent to solving a linear system—a theme revisited in change-of-basis matrices (Chapter 3) and diagonalization (Chapter 7).

Connections forward and exam strategy

Everything in this chapter is scaffolding for what follows. Subspaces reappear as column spaces, null spaces, and eigenspaces in Chapters 5 and 7. Span and linear independence are the language of basis construction, which enables coordinate representations and matrix factorizations. The subspace test is used implicitly every time we claim a solution set is a vector space. On exams, expect questions that ask you to verify a subset is a subspace, compute a span, or find coordinates relative to a non-standard basis—all requiring the exact step-by-step reasoning demonstrated above. Memorizing definitions without practicing the verification ritual will lead to lost points; the grader looks for explicit checks of all three subspace conditions, not just a conclusion.

Edge case reminder: Infinite-dimensional spaces (e.g., the space of all polynomials, or continuous functions on \([0,1]\)) satisfy all ten axioms but have no finite basis. This book focuses on finite-dimensional spaces, but be aware that some theorems (e.g., “every subspace has a basis”) require the axiom of choice in the infinite case. Exam questions will specify finite-dimensionality unless stated otherwise.
