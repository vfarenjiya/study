10. LU Decomposition

Chapter 10 of 11 22 min read

From algorithm to factorization

In Chapter 4, we performed Gaussian elimination as a sequence of row operations on the augmented matrix \([A \mid b]\). Each operation was equivalent to left-multiplication by an elementary matrix (Chapter 3), but we never wrote those matrices down explicitly. This chapter makes them explicit, packaging the entire elimination process into a single matrix factorization:

\[ A = LU \]

where \(L\) is lower triangular with 1s on the diagonal (unit lower triangular) and \(U\) is upper triangular. This is not merely notation — it is a reorganization of computation that separates the expensive part (factorization, done once) from the cheap part (solving with \(L\) and \(U\), done many times). When you need to solve \(Ax = b\) for hundreds of different right-hand sides \(b\) (as in time-stepping PDEs, optimization iterations, or Monte Carlo simulations), LU decomposition reduces the per-solve cost from \(O(n^3)\) to \(O(n^2)\).

This chapter derives \(A = LU\) constructively from elimination, explains when it exists and when it fails, introduces pivoting for numerical stability, and analyzes the computational cost that makes LU the default solver in scientific computing.

Deriving \(A = LU\) from elimination without row swaps

Assume for now that Gaussian elimination on \(A\) proceeds without row swaps and without encountering zero pivots. At step \(k\), we eliminate entries below the pivot \(a_{kk}^{(k)}\) by subtracting multiples of row \(k\) from rows \(k+1, \dots, n\). The multiplier for row \(i\) is

\[ \ell_{ik} = \frac{a_{ik}^{(k)}}{a_{kk}^{(k)}} \]

This row operation is left-multiplication by the elementary matrix \(E_k = I - \ell_k e_k^\top\), where \(\ell_k = (0, \dots, 0, \ell_{k+1,k}, \dots, \ell_{n,k})^\top\) has the multipliers below position \(k\). After \(n-1\) steps:

\[ E_{n-1} \cdots E_2 E_1 A = U \]

where \(U\) is upper triangular. Since each \(E_k\) is invertible with inverse \(E_k^{-1} = I + \ell_k e_k^\top\) (just flip the sign of the multipliers), we get

\[ A = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} \cdots E_{n-1}^{-1} U \]

The key insight: the product \(L = E_1^{-1} E_2^{-1} \cdots E_{n-1}^{-1}\) is unit lower triangular, with the multipliers \(\ell_{ik}\) appearing directly in positions \((i,k)\) below the diagonal. No multiplication needed — the factors assemble themselves.

Why \(L\) has this simple structure

Each \(E_k^{-1} = I + \ell_k e_k^\top\) differs from identity only in column \(k\) below the diagonal. When multiplying such matrices left-to-right, the nontrivial columns don't interfere: column \(j\) of \(E_j^{-1}\) is untouched by subsequent \(E_k^{-1}\) for \(k > j\) (since those only modify columns \(k > j\)). So the product \(L\) simply collects all the multiplier columns side by side:

\[ L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots \\ \ell_{21} & 1 & 0 & \cdots \\ \ell_{31} & \ell_{32} & 1 & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{pmatrix} \]

This is why LU decomposition is so elegant: the multipliers computed during elimination are the entries of \(L\). You don't compute \(L\) separately; you record it as you go.

Worked example: LU of a \(3 \times 3\) matrix

Factor \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix}\).

Step 1: Pivot \(a_{11} = 2\). Multipliers: \(\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2\), \(\ell_{31} = 8/2 = 4\). Eliminate:

\[ A^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \]

Step 2: Pivot \(a_{22}^{(2)} = 1\). Multiplier: \(\ell_{32} = 3/1 = 3\). Eliminate:

\[ U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \]

Assemble \(L\): Place multipliers in their positions:

\[ L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

Verify: \(LU = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix} = A\). ✓

Solving \(Ax = b\) via LU

Once \(A = LU\) is computed, solving \(Ax = b\) becomes two triangular solves:

\[ LUx = b \implies \text{let } y = Ux, \text{ solve } Ly = b \text{ (forward substitution)}, \text{ then solve } Ux = y \text{ (back substitution)} \]

Forward substitution exploits the lower-triangular structure of \(L\): \(y_1 = b_1\) (since \(\ell_{11}=1\)), then \(y_2 = b_2 - \ell_{21}y_1\), etc. Back substitution exploits upper-triangular \(U\): \(x_n = y_n / u_{nn}\), then \(x_{n-1} = (y_{n-1} - u_{n-1,n}x_n)/u_{n-1,n-1}\), etc. Both are \(O(n^2)\) — dramatically cheaper than the \(O(n^3)\) factorization.

Worked example: solving with the LU above

Solve \(Ax = b\) with \(b = (1, 1, 1)^\top\), using the \(L, U\) from above.

Forward solve \(Ly = b\):

\[ y_1 = 1; \quad y_2 = 1 - 2(1) = -1; \quad y_3 = 1 - 4(1) - 3(-1) = 0 \]

So \(y = (1, -1, 0)^\top\).

Back solve \(Ux = y\):

\[ x_3 = 0/2 = 0; \quad x_2 = (-1 - 1 \cdot 0)/1 = -1; \quad x_1 = (1 - 1 \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot 0)/2 = 1 \]

Solution: \(x = (1, -1, 0)^\top\). Verify: \(Ax = (2-1+0, 4-3+0, 8-7+0)^\top = (1,1,1)^\top = b\). ✓

When LU fails: zero pivots and the need for permutations

LU decomposition without row swaps exists if and only if all leading principal minors of \(A\) are nonzero (equivalently, all pivots encountered during elimination are nonzero). When a zero pivot appears, elimination stalls — but the matrix may still be invertible. The fix is partial pivoting: at each step, swap the current row with a row below that has the largest absolute value in the pivot column.

PA = LU: factorization with pivoting

Row swaps are encoded by a permutation matrix \(P\) (orthogonal, with exactly one 1 per row/column). With partial pivoting, elimination produces:

\[ PA = LU \]

where \(P\) records all row swaps performed during elimination. This factorization exists for every invertible matrix (and more generally, for every matrix with full rank). Solving proceeds as before: \(PAx = Pb \implies LUx = Pb\), forward/back substitute with the permuted right-hand side.

Why partial pivoting matters numerically

Even when no exact zero pivot occurs, small pivots cause catastrophic numerical instability. If \(|a_{kk}^{(k)}|\) is tiny, the multipliers \(\ell_{ik}\) become huge, amplifying rounding errors. Partial pivoting ensures \(|\ell_{ik}| \leq 1\) for all \(i, k\), bounding error growth. Complete pivoting (swapping both rows and columns) gives even better stability but is rarely worth the overhead; partial pivoting suffices for virtually all practical problems.

Worked example: LU with pivoting

Factor \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Without pivoting, the first pivot is 0 — failure. With partial pivoting: swap rows 1 and 2 (largest entry in column 1 is 1, in row 2). Permutation: \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\).

\[ PA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \]

This is already upper triangular, so \(U = PA\) and \(L = I\). Factorization: \(PA = LU\) with \(L = I\), \(U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). ✓

Computational cost: why LU dominates

The LU factorization of an \(n \times n\) matrix requires approximately \(\frac{2}{3}n^3\) floating-point operations (flops). Forward/back substitution each require \(n^2\) flops. For large \(n\), the factorization dominates: solving for 100 right-hand sides costs \(\frac{2}{3}n^3 + 100 \cdot 2n^2 \approx \frac{2}{3}n^3\) for large \(n\) — essentially the same as a single solve. This is why LU is the standard direct solver: factor once, solve many times cheaply.

Compare to computing \(A^{-1}\) explicitly: forming \(A^{-1}\) costs \(\sim 2n^3\) flops (via LU + \(n\) back-substitutions for columns of \(I\)), and then each solve \(A^{-1}b\) costs \(n^2\) — same asymptotic cost per solve, but with larger constant and worse numerical stability. Never compute \(A^{-1}\) explicitly to solve linear systems. Use LU (or Cholesky for symmetric positive definite, or QR for least squares).

Variants and special structures

  • Cholesky decomposition: If \(A\) is symmetric positive definite (Chapter 9), then \(A = LL^\top\) where \(L\) is lower triangular with positive diagonal. Cost: \(\frac{1}{3}n^3\) flops (half of LU). Stability: unconditional (no pivoting needed). This is the preferred solver for covariance matrices, normal equations, and energy minimization.
  • LDL\(^\top\) decomposition: For symmetric indefinite matrices, \(A = LDL^\top\) where \(D\) is block-diagonal with \(1 \times 1\) and \(2 \times 2\) blocks. Avoids square roots (unlike Cholesky) while preserving symmetry. Used in optimization (KKT systems) and structural mechanics.
  • Banded and sparse LU: When \(A\) has structure (banded, sparse), specialized orderings (minimum degree, nested dissection) reduce fill-in during factorization. Sparse LU is the engine behind finite element solvers, circuit simulation, and network analysis.
  • Block LU: Partition \(A\) into blocks and apply the same formulas at the block level (Chapter 3). Enables cache-efficient implementations on modern hardware by maximizing BLAS-3 operations (matrix-matrix multiplies).

Connection to other chapters

  • Chapter 4 (Gaussian elimination): LU is elimination packaged as a factorization. RREF is for theoretical analysis; LU is for computation.
  • Chapter 5 (Determinants): \(\det(A) = \det(L)\det(U) = \prod u_{ii}\) (since \(\det(L)=1\)). Determinant from LU is \(O(n^3)\) vs. \(O(n!)\) for cofactor expansion.
  • Chapter 9 (Quadratic forms): Cholesky (\(A=LL^\top\)) exists iff \(A\) is positive definite — a computational test for definiteness that avoids eigenvalue computation.
  • Chapter 11 (SVD): SVD is more stable than LU for rank-deficient or ill-conditioned problems, but costs \(\sim 21n^3\) flops. Use LU for well-conditioned square systems; SVD for least squares, rank estimation, and pseudoinverses.

Edge cases and common mistakes

Mistake: computing \(A^{-1}\) to solve \(Ax=b\). As noted above, this is slower and less stable than LU. The only legitimate use of \(A^{-1}\) is theoretical; computationally, always factor.

Mistake: forgetting the permutation in \(PA=LU\). When solving with pivoted LU, you must permute \(b\) first: solve \(LUx = Pb\), not \(LUx = b\). Forgetting \(P\) gives wrong answers. Always store \(P\) alongside \(L, U\).

Mistake: assuming LU exists without pivoting. Many invertible matrices require pivoting (e.g., any matrix with \(a_{11}=0\)). Always use partial pivoting in practice. The unpivoted \(A=LU\) is primarily pedagogical.

Edge case: singular matrices. If \(A\) is singular, elimination produces a zero row in \(U\). LU factorization still exists (with \(U\) singular), but the system \(Ax=b\) may be inconsistent or have infinitely many solutions. Rank-revealing variants (RRQR, SVD) handle this robustly.

Numerical caution: Even with partial pivoting, LU can fail for extremely ill-conditioned matrices. Growth factor \(\rho = \max |u_{ij}| / \max |a_{ij}|\) measures instability; for random matrices \(\rho\) is modest, but pathological examples exist (Wilkinson matrix). In practice, check the condition number estimate after factorization; if too large, switch to QR or SVD.

What we built, and where it goes

  • LU decomposition packages Gaussian elimination as \(A = LU\) (or \(PA = LU\) with pivoting), separating expensive factorization from cheap solves.
  • \(L\) stores elimination multipliers; \(U\) is the row echelon form. Both are triangular, enabling \(O(n^2)\) forward/back substitution.
  • Partial pivoting ensures numerical stability by bounding multiplier growth; it is mandatory in practice.
  • Cholesky exploits symmetry and positive definiteness for half-cost, unconditionally stable factorization.
  • LU is the computational backbone connecting elimination (Ch. 4), determinants (Ch. 5), quadratic forms (Ch. 9), and serving as baseline for SVD (Ch. 11).

Pencil-and-paper checkpoint: given a \(3 \times 3\) matrix, can you compute its LU factorization by hand, recording multipliers as you eliminate? Can you solve \(Ax=b\) using forward/back substitution without recomputing the factorization? Can you explain why partial pivoting is necessary and how \(P\) modifies the solve?

Chapter 11, the final chapter, unifies everything: the singular value decomposition generalizes eigen-decomposition to arbitrary (non-square, non-symmetric) matrices, provides optimal low-rank approximation, defines the pseudoinverse for least squares, and reveals the true numerical rank. LU solves well-conditioned square systems; SVD solves everything else. Together, they complete the computational toolkit of linear algebra.
