3. Matrices, Operations, and Special Types

Chapter 3 of 14 17 min read

Matrices as linear maps and data structures

In Chapter 2 we established that every finite-dimensional vector space is isomorphic to \(F^n\) once a basis is chosen. Matrices are the concrete embodiment of this isomorphism: they encode linear transformations between coordinate spaces, store systems of equations, and represent quadratic forms, graphs, and datasets. But a matrix is not merely a rectangular array of numbers—it is an algebraic object with its own arithmetic, structural properties, and geometric meaning. Confusing the array with the map it represents is the single most common source of error in linear algebra exams and applications alike.

This chapter builds the complete algebraic machinery: addition, scalar multiplication, matrix multiplication, transpose, inverse, and special classes (symmetric, orthogonal, idempotent, projection). Each operation is defined precisely, motivated by what it represents geometrically or computationally, and illustrated with fully worked numerical examples. The goal is not just to memorize rules but to understand why matrix multiplication is defined as row-times-column (it encodes composition of linear maps), why transposes appear in orthogonality conditions (they encode adjoints), and why invertibility is equivalent to column independence (linking back to Chapter 2). These connections make the entire subject coherent rather than a collection of isolated formulas.

Matrix arithmetic: addition, scaling, and multiplication

An \(m \times n\) matrix over a field \(F\) is a rectangular array \(A = [a_{ij}]\) with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, where each \(a_{ij} \in F\). Matrix addition and scalar multiplication are defined componentwise, exactly like vectors:

\[ (A + B)_{ij} = a_{ij} + b_{ij}, \quad (cA)_{ij} = c \cdot a_{ij}. \]

These operations make the set \(M_{m \times n}(F)\) of all \(m \times n\) matrices into a vector space of dimension \(mn\). The zero vector is the zero matrix \(\mathbf{0}_{m \times n}\), and additive inverses are computed componentwise. This vector space structure is used implicitly whenever we speak of “the space of matrices” or take linear combinations of matrices.

Matrix multiplication is fundamentally different. If \(A\) is \(m \times n\) and \(B\) is \(n \times p\), then \(AB\) is the \(m \times p\) matrix with entries:

\[ (AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b_{kj}. \]

This definition is not arbitrary: it encodes composition of linear maps. If \(T: F^n \to F^m\) has matrix \(A\) and \(S: F^p \to F^n\) has matrix \(B\) (both relative to standard bases), then \(T \circ S: F^p \to F^m\) has matrix \(AB\). The inner dimension \(n\) must match because the output space of \(S\) must equal the input space of \(T\). This compositional interpretation explains why matrix multiplication is associative (\((AB)C = A(BC)\)) but not commutative (\(AB \neq BA\) in general): function composition is associative but order-dependent.

Common mistake: Students frequently assume \(AB = BA\) or that \(AB = \mathbf{0}\) implies \(A = \mathbf{0}\) or \(B = \mathbf{0}\). Neither holds. For example, let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(AB = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) but \(BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \neq AB\), and neither factor is zero. Always verify commutativity claims with explicit computation or theorem.

Worked example: verifying associativity numerically

Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\), \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Verify \((AB)C = A(BC)\).

Step 1 (Compute \(AB\)):

\[ AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1\cdot0 + 2\cdot1 & 1\cdot1 + 2\cdot0 \\ 3\cdot0 + 4\cdot1 & 3\cdot1 + 4\cdot0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 2 (Compute \((AB)C\)):

\[ (AB)C = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2\cdot2 + 1\cdot(-1) & 2\cdot0 + 1\cdot1 \\ 4\cdot2 + 3\cdot(-1) & 4\cdot0 + 3\cdot1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 3 (Compute \(BC\)):

\[ BC = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0\cdot2 + 1\cdot(-1) & 0\cdot0 + 1\cdot1 \\ 1\cdot2 + 0\cdot(-1) & 1\cdot0 + 0\cdot1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 4 (Compute \(A(BC)\)):

\[ A(BC) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1\cdot(-1) + 2\cdot2 & 1\cdot1 + 2\cdot0 \\ 3\cdot(-1) + 4\cdot2 & 3\cdot1 + 4\cdot0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Conclusion: \((AB)C = A(BC) = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Associativity holds. Note that computing \(AB\) first versus \(BC\) first involves different intermediate matrices but yields the same final result—a direct consequence of function composition being well-defined regardless of grouping.

Transpose and symmetry

The transpose of an \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\), denoted \(A^\top\), is the \(n \times m\) matrix with \((A^\top)_{ij} = a_{ji}\). Geometrically, transposition swaps rows and columns; algebraically, it satisfies four key identities:

\[ (A + B)^\top = A^\top + B^\top, \quad (cA)^\top = cA^\top, \quad (AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top, \quad (A^\top)^\top = A. \]

The reversal in \((AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top\) mirrors the reversal in composition adjoints and is critical for deriving normal equations (Chapter 4), orthogonal projections (Chapter 11), and SVD (Chapter 13). Forgetting this reversal is among the most frequent algebraic errors in exams.

A square matrix \(A\) is symmetric if \(A^\top = A\), and skew-symmetric if \(A^\top = -A\). Symmetric matrices arise naturally as Gram matrices (\(X^\top X\)), Hessian matrices, and covariance matrices; they have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (Chapter 7), making them central to quadratic forms (Chapter 8) and optimization. Skew-symmetric matrices have zero diagonal and purely imaginary eigenvalues; they encode cross products and infinitesimal rotations.

Edge case: The zero matrix is both symmetric and skew-symmetric. Any square matrix can be uniquely decomposed as \(A = S + K\) where \(S = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^\top)\) is symmetric and \(K = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^\top)\) is skew-symmetric. This decomposition is used in physics (strain vs. rotation) and numerical analysis (symmetrization). Exams sometimes ask for this decomposition explicitly; know the formula cold.

Worked example: symmetric/skew decomposition

Decompose \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\) into symmetric and skew-symmetric parts.

Step 1 (Compute \(A^\top\)): \(A^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\).

Step 2 (Symmetric part):

\[ S = \tfrac{1}{2}(A + A^\top) = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 3 (Skew-symmetric part):

\[ K = \tfrac{1}{2}(A - A^\top) = \tfrac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 4 (Verify): \(S + K = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = A\). Check symmetry: \(S^\top = S\). Check skew: \(K^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -K\). Both conditions satisfied.

Sensitivity check: If \(A\) were already symmetric (e.g., \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\)), then \(K = \mathbf{0}\) and \(S = A\). If \(A\) were skew-symmetric (e.g., \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ -3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\)), then \(S = \mathbf{0}\) and \(K = A\). The decomposition adapts continuously to the structure of \(A\).

Invertibility and elementary matrices

A square matrix \(A \in M_n(F)\) is invertible (or nonsingular) if there exists \(A^{-1} \in M_n(F)\) such that \(AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I_n\). Invertibility is equivalent to any of the following (this equivalence theorem is one of the most important results in linear algebra and connects Chapters 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7):

\[ \begin{aligned} &A \text{ is invertible} \\ &\iff \det(A) \neq 0 \\ &\iff \operatorname{rank}(A) = n \\ &\iff \text{columns of } A \text{ are linearly independent} \\ &\iff Ax = \mathbf{0} \text{ has only the trivial solution} \\ &\iff Ax = b \text{ has a unique solution for every } b \in F^n \\ &\iff A \text{ is row-equivalent to } I_n \\ &\iff 0 \text{ is not an eigenvalue of } A. \end{aligned} \]

This equivalence means that invertibility can be checked via determinant (Chapter 6), rank (Chapter 5), row reduction (Chapter 4), or eigenvalues (Chapter 7)—whichever is most convenient for the problem at hand. On exams, recognizing which characterization applies saves significant time.

Elementary matrices encode row operations: swapping rows \(i\) and \(j\), multiplying row \(i\) by nonzero scalar \(c\), or adding \(c\) times row \(j\) to row \(i\). Each elementary matrix \(E\) is invertible, and left-multiplying \(A\) by \(E\) performs the corresponding row operation on \(A\). Crucially, \(A\) is invertible if and only if it can be written as a product of elementary matrices—this is the theoretical foundation of Gaussian elimination (Chapter 4) and LU decomposition (Chapter 12).

Derivation: inverse of a product. Let \(A\) and \(B\) be invertible \(n \times n\) matrices. Claim: \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\). Proof: Compute \((AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I\). Similarly, \((B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}IB = B^{-1}B = I\). Thus \(B^{-1}A^{-1}\) satisfies the definition of \((AB)^{-1}\). Note the reversal—this mirrors the transpose identity and reflects composition order. ∎

Worked example: computing inverse via augmented matrix

Find \(A^{-1}\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) using row reduction on \([A \mid I]\).

Step 1 (Augment): \(\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 5 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]\).

Step 2 (Eliminate below pivot): \(R_2 \gets R_2 - 3R_1\):

\[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & -3 & 1 \end{array}\right]. \]

Step 3 (Scale pivot row): \(R_2 \gets -R_2\):

\[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & -1 \end{array}\right]. \]

Step 4 (Eliminate above pivot): \(R_1 \gets R_1 - 2R_2\):

\[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & -5 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 & -1 \end{array}\right]. \]

Step 5 (Read off inverse): \(A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} -5 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\).

Step 6 (Verify): \(AA^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -5 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -5+6 & 2-2 \\ -15+15 & 6-5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Correct.

Sensitivity check: If we change \(A_{22}\) from 5 to 6, then \(\det(A) = 1\cdot6 - 2\cdot3 = 0\), so \(A\) becomes singular. Row reduction would yield a zero row on the left side of the augmented matrix, signaling non-invertibility. This sensitivity illustrates that invertibility is a codimension-1 condition: small perturbations can destroy it when \(\det(A)\) is near zero—a key insight for numerical stability in LU decomposition (Chapter 12).

Special matrices: orthogonal, idempotent, projection

Certain matrix classes recur throughout linear algebra because they encode fundamental geometric or algebraic structures. Understanding their defining properties and interrelationships is essential for later chapters.

An orthogonal matrix \(Q \in M_n(\mathbb{R})\) satisfies \(Q^\top Q = QQ^\top = I\). Equivalently, its columns form an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Orthogonal matrices preserve dot products and norms: \(\|Qx\| = \|x\|\) and \((Qx)^\top(Qy) = x^\top y\). They satisfy \(Q^{-1} = Q^\top\) and \(\det(Q) = \pm 1\). Orthogonal matrices are the building blocks of QR factorization, SVD (Chapter 13), and spectral decomposition (Chapter 7).

An idempotent matrix \(P\) satisfies \(P^2 = P\). Idempotent matrices are always diagonalizable with eigenvalues in \(\{0, 1\}\); their rank equals their trace (since trace = sum of eigenvalues = number of 1s = rank). Every idempotent matrix is a projection onto its range along its null space.

A projection matrix is an idempotent matrix. An orthogonal projection matrix is a symmetric idempotent matrix (\(P^2 = P\) and \(P^\top = P\)). Orthogonal projections project onto a subspace along its orthogonal complement; they minimize distance to the subspace (Chapter 11). The formula for orthogonal projection onto the column space of \(A\) (with full column rank) is \(P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\)—derived in Chapter 11 and used extensively in least squares (Chapter 4) and SVD (Chapter 13).

Common mistake: Not all projections are orthogonal. The matrix \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) satisfies \(P^2 = P\) but \(P^\top \neq P\); it projects onto the \(x\)-axis along the line \(y = -x\), not orthogonally. Only symmetric idempotents give orthogonal projections. Confusing these leads to incorrect least-squares solutions and misinterpreted residuals.

Worked example: verifying orthogonal projection

Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Compute the orthogonal projection matrix onto \(\operatorname{col}(A)\) and verify it is symmetric and idempotent.

Step 1 (Compute \(A^\top A\)): \(A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = 2\).

Step 2 (Compute \((A^\top A)^{-1}\)): \((A^\top A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\).

Step 3 (Compute \(P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\)):

\[ P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 4 (Verify symmetry): \(P^\top = P\). ✓

Step 5 (Verify idempotence):

\[ P^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.25+0.25 & 0.25+0.25 \\ 0.25+0.25 & 0.25+0.25 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix} = P. \quad \checkmark \]

Step 6 (Geometric check): Apply \(P\) to \(v = (3, 1)^\top\): \(Pv = (2, 2)^\top\). This is indeed the orthogonal projection of \((3,1)\) onto the line \(y = x\): the closest point on \(y=x\) to \((3,1)\) is \(((3+1)/2, (3+1)/2) = (2,2)\). Residual \((3,1)-(2,2)=(1,-1)\) is orthogonal to \((1,1)\): dot product = \(1 - 1 = 0\). All checks pass.

Connections forward and exam strategy

Matrix algebra is the language of the rest of this book. Gaussian elimination (Chapter 4) uses elementary matrices to factor \(A = LU\) (Chapter 12). Rank and nullity (Chapter 5) are defined via matrix properties. Determinants (Chapter 6) test invertibility. Eigenvalues (Chapter 7) solve \(Av = \lambda v\). Quadratic forms (Chapter 8) use symmetric matrices. Projections (Chapter 11) use orthogonal projection matrices. SVD (Chapter 13) factors any matrix as \(U\Sigma V^\top\) with orthogonal \(U, V\). Mastery of this chapter’s definitions and identities is non-negotiable for success in later chapters and on exams.

On exams, expect questions testing: (1) matrix multiplication associativity/non-commutativity, (2) transpose identities (especially \((AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top\)), (3) invertibility equivalences, (4) symmetric/skew decomposition, (5) verification of orthogonal/idempotent/projection properties. For each, show complete intermediate steps—not just final answers. Graders look for correct application of definitions, not pattern matching. Practice the worked examples above until you can reproduce them without reference; then vary the numbers to build fluency.

Edge case reminder: Non-square matrices cannot be invertible (only square matrices have two-sided inverses). However, they may have left or right inverses under rank conditions: an \(m \times n\) matrix with \(m > n\) and full column rank has a left inverse \((A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\); one with \(m < n\) and full row rank has a right inverse \(A^\top(AA^\top)^{-1}\). These generalized inverses appear in least squares (Chapter 4) and pseudoinverses (Chapter 13). Know the distinction between true inverses and one-sided inverses—exams frequently test this boundary.
