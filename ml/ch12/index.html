12. Clustering: k-Means & k-Medoid

Chapter 12 of 14 25 min read

Learning without labels

Every method in Chapters 1–11 was supervised: we had labeled training data \(\{(x_i, y_i)\}\) and learned a mapping from inputs to outputs. But in many real-world settings, labels are unavailable, expensive, or undefined. A biologist has gene expression profiles for thousands of samples but no prior classification. A marketer has customer purchase histories but no predefined segments. An astronomer has millions of stellar spectra but no catalog of star types.

The question clustering answers is: can we discover meaningful group structure in unlabeled data, and what does "meaningful" even mean without ground truth?

Unlike supervised learning, there is no single correct answer in clustering. Different algorithms encode different notions of what a "cluster" is. k-Means defines clusters as compact, spherical groups centered around prototypes. k-Medoid defines them similarly but restricts centers to actual data points, making it robust to outliers. Hierarchical clustering (Chapter 13) defines clusters via nested merge/split operations. The choice of algorithm is itself a modeling decision about the nature of the structure you expect.

The k-Means objective function

Given \(n\) data points \(x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and a desired number of clusters \(k\), k-means seeks to minimize the within-cluster sum of squared distances:

\[J(c, \mu) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i: c_i = j} \|x_i - \mu_j\|^2\]

where \(c_i \in \{1, \ldots, k\}\) is the cluster assignment for point \(i\), and \(\mu_j \in \mathbb{R}^p\) is the centroid (prototype) of cluster \(j\). The objective measures total compactness: lower \(J\) means points are closer to their assigned centroids.

This is a combinatorial optimization problem over assignments \(c\) and continuous optimization over centroids \(\mu\). Joint minimization is NP-hard in general. But conditional on one set of variables, the other has a closed-form solution — this enables coordinate descent.

Lloyd's algorithm: alternating optimization

Lloyd's algorithm alternates two steps until convergence:

Step 1 (Assignment): Fix centroids \(\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_k\). Assign each point to the nearest centroid:

\[c_i \leftarrow \arg\min_{j \in \{1,\ldots,k\}} \|x_i - \mu_j\|^2\]

This step minimizes \(J\) with respect to \(c\) while holding \(\mu\) fixed. Each point independently chooses its closest centroid, so this is exact and takes \(O(nkp)\) time.

Step 2 (Update): Fix assignments \(c_1, \ldots, c_n\). Update each centroid to the mean of its assigned points:

\[\mu_j \leftarrow \frac{1}{|S_j|} \sum_{i \in S_j} x_i, \quad \text{where } S_j = \{i : c_i = j\}\]

This step minimizes \(J\) with respect to \(\mu\) while holding \(c\) fixed. Why is the mean optimal? For any fixed set \(S_j\), the quantity \(\sum_{i \in S_j} \|x_i - \mu_j\|^2\) is minimized at \(\mu_j = \frac{1}{|S_j|}\sum_{i \in S_j} x_i\) — this is exactly the result from Chapter 1 showing that the mean minimizes squared error. If \(S_j = \emptyset\), the centroid is undefined; common fixes include reinitializing to a random point or keeping the previous value.

Convergence guarantees and limitations

Each step of Lloyd's algorithm decreases (or maintains) \(J\). Since \(J \geq 0\) and there are finitely many distinct assignments (\(k^n\) at most), the algorithm must converge in finite iterations. However:

  • Local optima: Convergence is to a local minimum of \(J\), not necessarily the global minimum. Different initializations yield different solutions.
  • Initialization sensitivity: Poor initialization can produce arbitrarily bad local optima. k-means++ initialization (choosing initial centroids with probability proportional to squared distance from existing centroids) provides an \(O(\log k)\) approximation guarantee and dramatically improves practical performance.
  • Empty clusters: Points may abandon a centroid entirely during assignment, leaving \(|S_j| = 0\). This requires handling as noted above.

Worked example: k-Means on six points

Data in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): \(x_1=(1,1)\), \(x_2=(1,2)\), \(x_3=(2,1)\), \(x_4=(5,5)\), \(x_5=(5,6)\), \(x_6=(6,5)\). Set \(k=2\).

Initialization: Choose \(\mu_1^{(0)} = x_1 = (1,1)\), \(\mu_2^{(0)} = x_4 = (5,5)\).

Iteration 1 — Assignment:

Compute squared Euclidean distances from each point to both centroids:

Point \(d^2\) to \(\mu_1=(1,1)\) \(d^2\) to \(\mu_2=(5,5)\) Assign
\(x_1=(1,1)\)0321
\(x_2=(1,2)\)1251
\(x_3=(2,1)\)1251
\(x_4=(5,5)\)3202
\(x_5=(5,6)\)4112
\(x_6=(6,5)\)4112

Assignments: \(S_1 = \{1,2,3\}\), \(S_2 = \{4,5,6\}\).

Iteration 1 — Update:

\[\mu_1^{(1)} = \frac{(1,1)+(1,2)+(2,1)}{3} = \left(\frac{4}{3}, \frac{4}{3}\right) \approx (1.333, 1.333)\] \[\mu_2^{(1)} = \frac{(5,5)+(5,6)+(6,5)}{3} = \left(\frac{16}{3}, \frac{16}{3}\right) \approx (5.333, 5.333)\]

Iteration 2 — Assignment:

Recompute distances to new centroids. By symmetry, \(x_1,x_2,x_3\) remain closest to \(\mu_1^{(1)}\) and \(x_4,x_5,x_6\) to \(\mu_2^{(1)}\). Assignments unchanged → algorithm has converged.

Final result: Two clusters \(\{x_1,x_2,x_3\}\) and \(\{x_4,x_5,x_6\}\) with centroids \((4/3, 4/3)\) and \((16/3, 16/3)\). Final objective:

\[J = \underbrace{\|(1,1)-(4/3,4/3)\|^2 + \|(1,2)-(4/3,4/3)\|^2 + \|(2,1)-(4/3,4/3)\|^2}_{\text{Cluster 1}} + \underbrace{\|(5,5)-(16/3,16/3)\|^2 + \|(5,6)-(16/3,16/3)\|^2 + \|(6,5)-(16/3,16/3)\|^2}_{\text{Cluster 2}}\]

Cluster 1: \((1/9+1/9) + (1/9+4/9) + (4/9+1/9) = 2/9 + 5/9 + 5/9 = 12/9 = 4/3\).

Cluster 2: same by symmetry = \(4/3\).

Total \(J = 8/3 \approx 2.667\).

k-Medoid: robustness via data-point centers

k-Means uses the mean as cluster center, which is sensitive to outliers. A single extreme point can pull the centroid far from the bulk of the cluster. k-Medoid (specifically the PAM algorithm) restricts cluster centers to be actual data points (medoids), optimizing:

\[J_{\text{medoid}}(c, m) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \sum_{i: c_i = j} d(x_i, m_j)\]

where \(m_j \in \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}\) and \(d\) is any metric (not necessarily Euclidean). Using absolute distances instead of squared distances further reduces outlier sensitivity.

The PAM algorithm proceeds similarly to Lloyd's but with a crucial difference in the update step: instead of computing a mean, it searches among the assigned points for the one that minimizes within-cluster distance when chosen as medoid. This makes each update \(O(|S_j|^2 p)\) rather than \(O(|S_j| p)\), making k-medoid significantly more expensive than k-means for large clusters.

The advantage: k-medoid works with arbitrary distance metrics (Manhattan, cosine, edit distance) and produces interpretable representatives (actual data points rather than synthetic means). It is preferred when cluster centers need to be exemplars or when the data is non-Euclidean.

Worked example: k-Medoid vs. k-Means with an outlier

Data: \(x_1=(0,0)\), \(x_2=(1,0)\), \(x_3=(0,1)\), \(x_4=(100,100)\). Set \(k=2\).

k-Means (initialized with \(x_1, x_4\)): Cluster 1 gets \(\{x_1,x_2,x_3\}\) with centroid \((1/3, 1/3)\). Cluster 2 gets \(\{x_4\}\) with centroid \((100,100)\). The centroid \((1/3,1/3)\) is pulled slightly toward the origin by the three nearby points, but if \(x_4\) were \((10,10)\) instead, the boundary would shift significantly. The mean is always influenced by every point.

k-Medoid: Candidate medoids must be actual data points. For cluster \(\{x_1,x_2,x_3\}\), the total distance using each candidate:

  • \(m=x_1\): \(d(x_1,x_1)+d(x_2,x_1)+d(x_3,x_1) = 0+1+1 = 2\)
  • \(m=x_2\): \(1+0+\sqrt{2} \approx 2.414\)
  • \(m=x_3\): \(1+\sqrt{2}+0 \approx 2.414\)

\(x_1\) minimizes total distance and is selected as medoid. The outlier \(x_4\) forms its own cluster with itself as medoid. Crucially, moving \(x_4\) to \((1000,1000)\) changes nothing about cluster 1's medoid — k-medoid is completely unaffected by how far the outlier is, as long as it remains in its own cluster.

Choosing \(k\)

Neither k-means nor k-medoid determines \(k\) automatically. Common heuristics include:

  • Elbow method: Plot \(J\) vs. \(k\); choose \(k\) where the decrease sharply slows ("elbow"). Subjective but widely used.
  • Silhouette score: For each point, compute \(s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))}\) where \(a(i)\) is average intra-cluster distance and \(b(i)\) is average nearest-cluster distance. Average \(s(i)\) across all points; higher is better. Range \([-1, 1]\).
  • Domain knowledge: Often the most reliable guide. If you know there should be 3 customer segments, use \(k=3\).

Common mistake

Common mistake: Students often apply k-means to non-spherical or varying-density clusters and interpret the results as ground truth. k-Means implicitly assumes clusters are convex, isotropic, and roughly equal in size because it optimizes squared Euclidean distance to centroids. Elongated, nested, or irregularly shaped clusters will be poorly captured. Always visualize the resulting clusters and consider whether the spherical assumption is reasonable. If not, switch to density-based methods (DBSCAN), spectral clustering, or hierarchical clustering (Chapter 13). Treating k-means output as definitive rather than as one particular partitioning induced by one particular objective is a fundamental conceptual error.

Edge case

What happens when \(k = n\)? Each point is its own cluster. \(J = 0\) trivially. This is the degenerate minimum of the objective and illustrates why minimizing \(J\) alone is insufficient — we need external criteria (elbow, silhouette, domain knowledge) to select meaningful \(k\).

What happens when \(k = 1\)? All points belong to one cluster with centroid \(\bar{x}\). \(J = \sum_i \|x_i - \bar{x}\|^2 = \text{TSS}\), the total sum of squares. This is the maximum possible \(J\) for any \(k\) and serves as the baseline against which clustering improvement is measured.

What if multiple points are equidistant from two centroids? Tie-breaking is arbitrary and implementation-dependent. Different tie-breaking rules can lead to different final clusterings from the same initialization. In practice, exact distance ties are rare with floating-point data but can occur with integer or discretized features.

Where we go next

k-Means and k-medoid produce flat partitions: each point belongs to exactly one cluster, and all clusters exist at the same level of granularity. But many datasets have hierarchical structure: species within genera within families, documents within topics within super-topics. Chapter 13 introduces hierarchical clustering, which builds nested cluster trees via agglomerative (bottom-up) or divisive (top-down) strategies with various linkage criteria, producing dendrograms that reveal structure at every resolution simultaneously.
