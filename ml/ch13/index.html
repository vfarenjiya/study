13. Hierarchical Clustering

Chapter 13 of 14 25 min read

Beyond flat partitions

In Chapter 12, k-means and k-medoid produced a single flat partition: each point belongs to exactly one of \(k\) clusters, and all clusters exist at the same level of granularity. But many datasets have inherently nested structure. Biological taxonomy organizes species into genera, genera into families, families into orders. Document collections have subtopics within topics within domains. Customer segments may contain sub-segments that are meaningful at finer resolution.

The question hierarchical clustering answers is: can we discover cluster structure at every level of granularity simultaneously, without committing to a single value of \(k\)?

The answer is a dendrogram: a tree diagram where leaves are individual data points, internal nodes represent merges (or splits), and the height of each node encodes the dissimilarity at which the merge occurred. Cutting the dendrogram at any height yields a valid flat partition, so the user can choose resolution after seeing the full hierarchy rather than before.

Two strategies: bottom-up and top-down

There are two fundamental approaches to building hierarchical clusterings:

Agglomerative (bottom-up): Start with each point as its own cluster. At each step, merge the two most similar clusters. Continue until all points belong to a single cluster. This produces \(n-1\) merges for \(n\) points.

Divisive (top-down): Start with all points in one cluster. At each step, split the least cohesive cluster into two sub-clusters. Continue until every point is its own cluster.

Agglomerative methods are far more common in practice because they are computationally simpler (\(O(n^3)\) naive, \(O(n^2 \log n)\) with optimizations) and produce the same dendrogram regardless of tie-breaking order (under mild conditions). Divisive methods require solving a partitioning problem at each split, which is generally harder. We focus on agglomerative clustering below, noting that the GATE DA syllabus mentions both directions but emphasizes linkage criteria, which apply primarily to the agglomerative framework.

Linkage criteria: defining inter-cluster distance

The critical design choice in agglomerative clustering is how to measure the distance between two clusters (sets of points), given only pairwise distances between individual points. Three standard linkage criteria dominate:

Single linkage (minimum linkage)

\[d_{\text{single}}(C_a, C_b) = \min_{x_i \in C_a, x_j \in C_b} d(x_i, x_j)\]

The distance between two clusters is the distance between their closest pair of points. Single linkage favors merging clusters that are connected by even a single close pair, regardless of how far apart the bulk of the clusters are.

Complete linkage (maximum linkage)

\[d_{\text{complete}}(C_a, C_b) = \max_{x_i \in C_a, x_j \in C_b} d(x_i, x_j)\]

The distance between two clusters is the distance between their farthest pair of points. Complete linkage only merges clusters when all cross-pairs are close, favoring compact, spherical clusters of similar diameter.

Average linkage (UPGMA)

\[d_{\text{avg}}(C_a, C_b) = \frac{1}{|C_a||C_b|} \sum_{x_i \in C_a} \sum_{x_j \in C_b} d(x_i, x_j)\]

The distance between two clusters is the average of all cross-pair distances. Average linkage balances single and complete linkage, avoiding both chaining and excessive compactness bias.

These three criteria produce fundamentally different hierarchies from the same data. The choice encodes what you mean by "similar clusters" and should be guided by domain knowledge and exploratory analysis.

Worked example

Agglomerative clustering on five points with all three linkages

Data in \(\mathbb{R}^1\) (for simplicity): \(A=1\), \(B=2\), \(C=5\), \(D=8\), \(E=9\).

Pairwise distance matrix (absolute difference):

A B C D E
A01478
B10367
C43034
D76301
E87410

Single linkage

Step 1: Minimum pairwise distance is \(d(A,B)=1\) and \(d(D,E)=1\). Break tie arbitrarily; merge \(\{A,B\}\) first at height 1.

Step 2: Current clusters: \(\{A,B\}, \{C\}, \{D\}, \{E\}\). Compute single-linkage distances from \(\{A,B\}\) to others:

  • \(d_{\text{single}}(\{A,B\}, \{C\}) = \min(d(A,C), d(B,C)) = \min(4,3) = 3\)
  • \(d_{\text{single}}(\{A,B\}, \{D\}) = \min(7,6) = 6\)
  • \(d_{\text{single}}(\{A,B\}, \{E\}) = \min(8,7) = 7\)

Other pairwise: \(d(C,D)=3\), \(d(C,E)=4\), \(d(D,E)=1\). Minimum is \(d(D,E)=1\). Merge \(\{D,E\}\) at height 1.

Step 3: Current clusters: \(\{A,B\}, \{C\}, \{D,E\}\). Distances:

  • \(d_{\text{single}}(\{A,B\}, \{C\}) = 3\)
  • \(d_{\text{single}}(\{A,B\}, \{D,E\}) = \min(7,6,8,7) = 6\)
  • \(d_{\text{single}}(\{C\}, \{D,E\}) = \min(3,4) = 3\)

Minimum is 3 (tie between \(\{A,B\}\)-\(\{C\}\) and \(\{C\}\)-\(\{D,E\}\)). Merge \(\{A,B\}\) with \(\{C\}\) at height 3 → \(\{A,B,C\}\).

Step 4: Clusters: \(\{A,B,C\}, \{D,E\}\). Distance:

  • \(d_{\text{single}}(\{A,B,C\}, \{D,E\}) = \min(7,6,3,8,7,4) = 3\)

Merge at height 3. All points united.

Single-linkage dendrogram heights: \(\{A,B\}\) at 1, \(\{D,E\}\) at 1, \(\{A,B,C\}\) at 3, \(\{A,B,C,D,E\}\) at 3.

Complete linkage

Step 1: Same as single: merge \(\{A,B\}\) at height 1, then \(\{D,E\}\) at height 1.

Step 2: Current clusters: \(\{A,B\}, \{C\}, \{D,E\}\). Complete-linkage distances:

  • \(d_{\text{complete}}(\{A,B\}, \{C\}) = \max(d(A,C), d(B,C)) = \max(4,3) = 4\)
  • \(d_{\text{complete}}(\{A,B\}, \{D,E\}) = \max(7,6,8,7) = 8\)
  • \(d_{\text{complete}}(\{C\}, \{D,E\}) = \max(3,4) = 4\)

Minimum is 4 (tie). Merge \(\{A,B\}\) with \(\{C\}\) at height 4 → \(\{A,B,C\}\).

Step 3: Clusters: \(\{A,B,C\}, \{D,E\}\). Distance:

  • \(d_{\text{complete}}(\{A,B,C\}, \{D,E\}) = \max(7,6,3,8,7,4) = 8\)

Merge at height 8.

Complete-linkage dendrogram heights: \(\{A,B\}\) at 1, \(\{D,E\}\) at 1, \(\{A,B,C\}\) at 4, \(\{A,B,C,D,E\}\) at 8.

Key difference: Single linkage merged everything at height 3 because \(C\) was only distance 3 from \(D\). Complete linkage delayed the final merge to height 8 because \(A\) is distance 8 from \(E\). Single linkage sees connectivity; complete linkage sees diameter.

The chaining effect

Single linkage suffers from the chaining effect: it tends to produce elongated, straggly clusters because it only requires one close pair to merge. A chain of points spaced at distance 1 will be merged into a single cluster even if the endpoints are far apart, while two compact clusters separated by a gap slightly larger than 1 will remain separate. This makes single linkage sensitive to noise bridges between otherwise distinct groups.

Complete linkage avoids chaining but has the opposite problem: it can split naturally elongated or irregularly shaped clusters because it demands all cross-pairs be close. Average linkage provides a compromise that works well in many practical settings.

Reading the dendrogram

The dendrogram encodes the full hierarchy. To extract a flat partition with \(k\) clusters, cut the dendrogram at the height just below the \((k-1)\)-th highest merge. Large vertical gaps between consecutive merge heights suggest natural cluster boundaries: if merges occur at heights 1, 1.5, 2, and then jump to 10, cutting between 2 and 10 yields a stable partition. Small, uniform merge heights suggest no clear structure.

Importantly, the horizontal ordering of leaves in a dendrogram is arbitrary — only the vertical heights carry information. Rotating branches around internal nodes does not change the clustering.

Common mistake

Common mistake: Students often interpret the horizontal proximity of leaves in a dendrogram as indicating similarity. Two leaves drawn adjacent to each other horizontally may have been merged at a very high level; their adjacency is merely an artifact of the drawing convention. Only the vertical height of the lowest common ancestor encodes dissimilarity. Always read dendrograms vertically, never horizontally. A related mistake: assuming that hierarchical clustering produces a "correct" number of clusters. Unlike supervised learning with ground truth, the number of clusters is always a subjective judgment informed by the dendrogram's structure, domain knowledge, and downstream utility. There is no universally optimal \(k\).

Edge case

What happens when all pairwise distances are equal? Every merge occurs at the same height. The dendrogram is completely balanced (or unbalanced depending on tie-breaking), and no level of the hierarchy is preferred over any other. This correctly reflects the absence of structure: when all points are equidistant, there is no meaningful grouping. Any flat partition extracted from such a dendrogram is equally arbitrary.

What about computational complexity? Naive agglomerative clustering recomputes the full distance matrix after each merge, taking \(O(n^3)\). Optimizations like the SLINK algorithm (single linkage, \(O(n^2)\) time and space) and nearest-neighbor chain methods reduce this for specific linkages. For large \(n\), approximate methods or sampling-based approaches become necessary. This contrasts with k-means (Chapter 12), which scales linearly in \(n\) per iteration.

Where we go next

Hierarchical clustering reveals structure at every resolution but operates directly in the original feature space. When data lives in high dimensions, distances become less informative (the curse of dimensionality discussed in Chapter 5), and clustering degrades. Chapter 14 introduces Principal Component Analysis (PCA), which finds low-dimensional linear projections that preserve maximum variance, enabling effective visualization, denoising, and preprocessing for downstream clustering and classification.
