14. Dimensionality Reduction & Principal Component Analysis

Chapter 14 of 14 30 min read

The curse and the cure

In Chapter 5, we encountered the curse of dimensionality: as the number of features \(p\) grows, distances between points become uninformative, density estimation requires exponentially more data, and algorithms like kNN degrade. In Chapter 12, clustering in high dimensions produces misleading partitions because noise dimensions dominate distance calculations. Even supervised models suffer: with \(p > n\), OLS (Chapter 2) is undefined, LDA (Chapter 7) has singular covariance estimates, and overfitting becomes nearly inevitable.

The question dimensionality reduction answers is: can we find a lower-dimensional representation of the data that preserves the essential structure while discarding noise and redundancy?

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the foundational technique. It finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance in the data and projects onto the top \(k\) of them. Despite being entirely linear and unsupervised, PCA serves as preprocessing for virtually every downstream task in this book: visualization, denoising, compression, and improving the conditioning of supervised and unsupervised algorithms alike.

Two equivalent objectives

PCA can be motivated from two seemingly different perspectives that turn out to be mathematically equivalent. Understanding both deepens intuition.

Objective 1: Maximum projected variance

We seek a unit vector \(\mathbf{u}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^p\) such that projecting the centered data onto \(\mathbf{u}_1\) captures the maximum possible variance. Let \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) be the centered data matrix (each column has mean zero). The projection of all \(n\) points onto \(\mathbf{u}_1\) is \(X\mathbf{u}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^n\). Its sample variance is:

\[\frac{1}{n}(X\mathbf{u}_1)^\top (X\mathbf{u}_1) = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{u}_1^\top X^\top X \mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{u}_1^\top \hat{\Sigma} \mathbf{u}_1\]

where \(\hat{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X\) is the sample covariance matrix (using the centered data). We want to maximize this quadratic form subject to \(\|\mathbf{u}_1\| = 1\):

\[\max_{\mathbf{u}_1} \mathbf{u}_1^\top \hat{\Sigma} \mathbf{u}_1 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbf{u}_1^\top \mathbf{u}_1 = 1\]

Objective 2: Minimum reconstruction error

Alternatively, we seek a rank-\(k\) approximation \(\hat{X}\) of \(X\) that minimizes the total squared reconstruction error:

\[\min_{\text{rank}(\hat{X}) = k} \|X - \hat{X}\|_F^2\]

For \(k = 1\), \(\hat{X} = \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^\top X\) (projecting each row onto \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and back). The reconstruction error is:

\[\|X - \mathbf{u}_1 \mathbf{u}_1^\top X\|_F^2 = \|X\|_F^2 - \mathbf{u}_1^\top X^\top X \mathbf{u}_1\]

Since \(\|X\|_F^2\) is constant, minimizing reconstruction error is equivalent to maximizing \(\mathbf{u}_1^\top X^\top X \mathbf{u}_1\) — exactly Objective 1. Maximum variance and minimum reconstruction error are the same optimization problem.

Solving via eigendecomposition

The constrained maximization \(\max \mathbf{u}^\top \hat{\Sigma} \mathbf{u}\) subject to \(\|\mathbf{u}\| = 1\) is solved by Lagrange multipliers. The Lagrangian is:

\[\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{u}, \lambda) = \mathbf{u}^\top \hat{\Sigma} \mathbf{u} - \lambda(\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{u} - 1)\]

Taking the gradient with respect to \(\mathbf{u}\) and setting to zero:

\[2\hat{\Sigma}\mathbf{u} - 2\lambda \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0} \implies \hat{\Sigma}\mathbf{u} = \lambda \mathbf{u}\]

This is the eigenvalue equation! The optimal projection directions are the eigenvectors of \(\hat{\Sigma}\), and the corresponding eigenvalues \(\lambda\) equal the projected variance \(\mathbf{u}^\top \hat{\Sigma} \mathbf{u} = \lambda \mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{u} = \lambda\).

Since \(\hat{\Sigma}\) is symmetric positive semidefinite, its eigenvalues are real and non-negative: \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_p \geq 0\), with corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_p\). The first principal component is \(\mathbf{u}_1\) (direction of maximum variance \(\lambda_1\)), the second is \(\mathbf{u}_2\) (maximum variance among directions orthogonal to \(\mathbf{u}_1\)), and so on.

The \(k\)-dimensional PCA representation of point \(x_i\) is:

\[z_i = (\mathbf{u}_1^\top x_i, \mathbf{u}_2^\top x_i, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_k^\top x_i)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^k\]

The fraction of total variance retained by the first \(k\) components is:

\[\boxed{\text{Variance Retained} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k} \lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^{p} \lambda_j}}Worked example

PCA on four points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)

Data: \(x_1 = (1, 2)\), \(x_2 = (2, 4)\), \(x_3 = (3, 3)\), \(x_4 = (4, 5)\).

Step 1: Center the data.

\[\bar{x} = \left(\frac{1+2+3+4}{4}, \frac{2+4+3+5}{4}\right) = (2.5, 3.5)\]

Centered data matrix \(X\):

\[X = \begin{pmatrix} -1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 1.5 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix}\]

Step 2: Compute the covariance matrix.

\[\hat{\Sigma} = \frac{1}{4} X^\top X = \frac{1}{4} \begin{pmatrix} -1.5 & -0.5 & 0.5 & 1.5 \\ -1.5 & 0.5 & -0.5 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 1.5 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix}\]

Computing entries:

  • \((1,1)\): \(\frac{1}{4}(2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25) = \frac{5}{4} = 1.25\)
  • \((1,2)\): \(\frac{1}{4}(2.25 - 0.25 - 0.25 + 2.25) = \frac{4}{4} = 1.0\)
  • \((2,1)\): \(1.0\) (symmetric)
  • \((2,2)\): \(\frac{1}{4}(2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25) = 1.25\)
\[\hat{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.25 & 1.0 \\ 1.0 & 1.25 \end{pmatrix}\]

Step 3: Find eigenvalues.

Solve \(\det(\hat{\Sigma} - \lambda I) = 0\):

\[(1.25 - \lambda)^2 - 1.0^2 = 0 \implies (1.25 - \lambda)^2 = 1 \implies 1.25 - \lambda = \pm 1\] \[\lambda_1 = 1.25 + 1 = 2.25, \quad \lambda_2 = 1.25 - 1 = 0.25\]

Total variance: \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 2.5\).

Step 4: Find eigenvectors.

For \(\lambda_1 = 2.25\): solve \((\hat{\Sigma} - 2.25I)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\):

\[\begin{pmatrix} -1.0 & 1.0 \\ 1.0 & -1.0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0} \implies u_1 = u_2\]

Normalized: \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, 1)^\top \approx (0.707, 0.707)^\top\).

For \(\lambda_2 = 0.25\): solve \((\hat{\Sigma} - 0.25I)\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\):

\[\begin{pmatrix} 1.0 & 1.0 \\ 1.0 & 1.0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{0} \implies u_1 = -u_2\]

Normalized: \(\mathbf{u}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1, -1)^\top \approx (0.707, -0.707)^\top\).

Step 5: Interpret.

The first principal component \(\mathbf{u}_1\) points along the diagonal direction \((1,1)\), capturing variance \(\lambda_1 = 2.25\). The second PC \(\mathbf{u}_2\) is orthogonal, pointing along \((1,-1)\), capturing only \(\lambda_2 = 0.25\). Variance retained by PC1 alone:

\[\frac{2.25}{2.5} = 0.9 = 90\%\]

Projecting onto just the first PC retains 90% of the total variance, reducing dimensionality from 2 to 1 with minimal information loss.

Step 6: Project and reconstruct.

Projections onto PC1: \(z_i = \mathbf{u}_1^\top (x_i - \bar{x})\):

  • \(z_1 = 0.707(-1.5) + 0.707(-1.5) = -2.121\)
  • \(z_2 = 0.707(-0.5) + 0.707(0.5) = 0\)
  • \(z_3 = 0.707(0.5) + 0.707(-0.5) = 0\)
  • \(z_4 = 0.707(1.5) + 0.707(1.5) = 2.121\)

Reconstruction: \(\hat{x}_i = \bar{x} + z_i \mathbf{u}_1\):

  • \(\hat{x}_1 = (2.5, 3.5) + (-2.121)(0.707, 0.707) = (2.5 - 1.5, 3.5 - 1.5) = (1.0, 2.0)\)
  • \(\hat{x}_2 = (2.5, 3.5) + 0 = (2.5, 3.5)\)
  • \(\hat{x}_3 = (2.5, 3.5) + 0 = (2.5, 3.5)\)
  • \(\hat{x}_4 = (2.5, 3.5) + (2.121)(0.707, 0.707) = (4.0, 5.0)\)

Points \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) collapse to the same reconstruction because they differ only along PC2, which was discarded. The reconstruction error is entirely in the PC2 direction, totaling \(n \cdot \lambda_2 = 4 \times 0.25 = 1.0\).

Choosing the number of components

Common strategies include:

  • Variance threshold: Choose smallest \(k\) such that cumulative variance exceeds a target (e.g., 95%). Simple and interpretable.
  • Scree plot: Plot eigenvalues vs. component index; look for an "elbow" where eigenvalues drop sharply then flatten. Subjective but visually informative.
  • Kaiser criterion: Retain components with \(\lambda_j > 1\) (when using correlation matrix instead of covariance). Widely used in factor analysis but sometimes retains too many or too few components.
  • Cross-validation: When PCA is preprocessing for a downstream task, choose \(k\) by CV on the downstream metric (Chapter 10).

Whitening: decorrelating and rescaling

Standard PCA produces uncorrelated components with variances \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots\). Sometimes we want components with unit variance as well. PCA whitening transforms the projected data:

\[z_i^{\text{white}} = \Lambda_k^{-1/2} U_k^\top (x_i - \bar{x})\]

where \(\Lambda_k = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k)\) and \(U_k = [\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_k]\). The whitened representation has identity covariance, making it suitable for algorithms that assume spherical data (e.g., k-means, ICA). The tradeoff: whitening amplifies noise in low-variance directions by dividing by small \(\sqrt{\lambda_j}\), so it should be used judiciously.

Common mistake

Common mistake: Students often apply PCA without centering the data first. Without centering, the first principal component points toward the mean of the data rather than the direction of maximum variance. The covariance matrix \(X^\top X / n\) only equals the true sample covariance when \(X\) is centered; otherwise it includes a rank-1 contribution from the mean that dominates the eigendecomposition. Always subtract column means before computing PCA. A related mistake: interpreting principal components as meaningful latent variables. PCs are linear combinations optimized for variance, not interpretability. A PC might mix age, income, and height in proportions that maximize spread but have no semantic meaning. If interpretable factors are desired, consider factor analysis or domain-guided feature engineering instead.

Edge case

What happens when multiple eigenvalues are equal? The corresponding eigenspace is multidimensional, and any orthonormal basis within it is equally valid. Different software may return different eigenvectors for degenerate eigenvalues, but the subspace spanned is unique. The variance explained by the group of equal eigenvalues is well-defined even though individual components within the group are not. This occurs naturally when data has rotational symmetry (e.g., points uniformly distributed on a sphere).

What about nonlinear structure? PCA is fundamentally linear: it can only capture variance along straight-line directions. Data lying on a curved manifold (e.g., a Swiss roll) will not be well-represented by PCA even if the intrinsic dimensionality is low. Nonlinear extensions like kernel PCA, Isomap, and t-SNE address this, but they are beyond the GATE DA syllabus scope. Recognizing when PCA fails (reconstruction error remains high despite many components, or visualizations show curved patterns) is as important as knowing how to apply it.

Completing the syllabus

With this chapter, we have covered every topic on the GATE DA machine learning syllabus: supervised learning (regression, classification, SVMs, decision trees, neural networks), model evaluation (bias-variance, cross-validation), and unsupervised learning (clustering, dimensionality reduction). The threads connecting these topics are the bias-variance tradeoff (Chapter 10), which governs every hyperparameter choice; the geometry of high-dimensional spaces, which motivates both regularization and dimensionality reduction; and the principle that every algorithm encodes assumptions about data structure that must be matched to the problem at hand. Mastery comes not from memorizing formulas but from understanding why each method exists, what it assumes, and when it fails.
