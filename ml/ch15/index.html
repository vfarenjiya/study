15. Clustering: k-Means and k-Medoids

Chapter 15 of 17 24 min read

Unsupervised learning and the clustering objective

Unlike supervised learning (Chapters 1–14), clustering operates without labels. Given \(n\) data points \(\{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^p\), the goal is to partition them into \(k\) groups such that points within each group are more similar to each other than to points in other groups. This is inherently ill-posed: there is no ground truth to optimize against, and "good" clustering depends entirely on the application context. Nevertheless, formalizing similarity via an objective function enables principled algorithms with provable properties.

The k-means objective (also called within-cluster sum of squares or distortion) seeks cluster centers \(\boldsymbol\mu_1, \dots, \boldsymbol\mu_k \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and assignments \(r_{ik} \in \{0,1\}\) minimizing:

\[ J(\{\boldsymbol\mu_j\}, \{r_{ij}\}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{k} r_{ij} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_j\|^2 \]

subject to \(\sum_j r_{ij} = 1\) for all \(i\) (each point assigned to exactly one cluster). This is equivalent to finding the best piecewise-constant approximation of the data under squared Euclidean distance. Minimizing \(J\) is NP-hard in general, but Lloyd's algorithm finds a local minimum efficiently via coordinate descent. The key insight is that for fixed assignments, optimal centers are cluster means; for fixed centers, optimal assignments are nearest-center rules. Alternating these two steps monotonically decreases \(J\) until convergence.

Common mistake: treating k-means output as ground truth. K-means finds a local minimum of \(J\), not the global optimum. Different initializations yield different partitions, none of which may correspond to semantically meaningful groups. Always run multiple restarts, visualize results, and validate against domain knowledge or downstream task performance rather than trusting \(J\) alone. Also beware that minimizing within-cluster variance implicitly assumes spherical, equally-sized clusters; elongated or imbalanced structures are poorly captured regardless of \(k\).

Lloyd’s algorithm: derivation and convergence

Lloyd's algorithm alternates two steps until convergence:

Assignment step (E-step analogue)

For fixed centers \(\{\boldsymbol\mu_j\}\), assign each point to its nearest center:

\[ r_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } j = \arg\min_\ell \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_\ell\|^2 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

This minimizes \(J\) w.r.t. \(\{r_{ij}\}\) because each term \(r_{ij}\|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_j\|^2\) is independently minimized by choosing the smallest distance. Ties are broken arbitrarily (deterministically or randomly).

Update step (M-step analogue)

For fixed assignments \(\{r_{ij}\}\), set each center to the mean of its assigned points:

\[ \boldsymbol\mu_j = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ij} \mathbf{x}_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ij}} \]

This minimizes \(J\) w.r.t. \(\{\boldsymbol\mu_j\}\) because the mean is the unique minimizer of \(\sum_i \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu\|^2\) (derivative = 0 at sample mean; second derivative positive definite). Empty clusters (\(\sum_i r_{ij} = 0\)) require special handling: reinitialize to a random point, split the largest cluster, or assign the farthest point from any existing center.

Convergence guarantee

Each step strictly decreases \(J\) unless already at a stationary point. Since \(J \geq 0\) and there are finitely many distinct partitions (\(k^n\) upper bound), Lloyd's algorithm converges to a local minimum in finite iterations. In practice, convergence occurs in tens to hundreds of iterations for moderate \(n, k\). However, the number of possible local minima can be exponential, and the quality of the solution depends critically on initialization.

Edge case: when two or more centers coincide, the assignment step creates degenerate clusters that may persist indefinitely. Perturbing coincident centers slightly after each update prevents stagnation. Also note that Lloyd's algorithm can cycle between partitions with identical \(J\) values in pathological cases (e.g., symmetric configurations); deterministic tie-breaking rules prevent infinite loops but don't guarantee uniqueness of the limit point.

Worked example: hand-computed k-means iteration

Data: six points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): \(A=(1,1), B=(1,2), C=(2,1), D=(5,5), E=(5,6), F=(6,5)\). Set \(k=2\). Initialize centers: \(\boldsymbol\mu_1^{(0)} = (1,1)\) (point A), \(\boldsymbol\mu_2^{(0)} = (5,5)\) (point D).

Iteration 1, Assignment: Compute squared distances to each center:

\[ \begin{array}{c|cc} \text{Point} & \|\cdot - \boldsymbol\mu_1\|^2 & \|\cdot - \boldsymbol\mu_2\|^2 \\ \hline A(1,1) & 0 & 32 \\ B(1,2) & 1 & 25 \\ C(2,1) & 1 & 25 \\ D(5,5) & 32 & 0 \\ E(5,6) & 41 & 1 \\ F(6,5) & 41 & 1 \end{array} \]

Assignments: Cluster 1 = \(\{A,B,C\}\), Cluster 2 = \(\{D,E,F\}\).

Iteration 1, Update:

\[ \boldsymbol\mu_1^{(1)} = \left(\frac{1+1+2}{3}, \frac{1+2+1}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{4}{3}, \frac{4}{3}\right) \approx (1.33, 1.33) \] \[ \boldsymbol\mu_2^{(1)} = \left(\frac{5+5+6}{3}, \frac{5+6+5}{3}\right) = \left(\frac{16}{3}, \frac{16}{3}\right) \approx (5.33, 5.33) \]

Iteration 2, Assignment: Recompute distances to new centers. Point B: to \(\boldsymbol\mu_1^{(1)}\): \((1-1.33)^2+(2-1.33)^2 = 0.11+0.45=0.56\); to \(\boldsymbol\mu_2^{(1)}\): \((1-5.33)^2+(2-5.33)^2 = 18.75+11.09=29.84\). Still cluster 1. All assignments unchanged → converged. Final \(J = 0 + 0.56 + 0.56 + 0 + 0.56 + 0.56 = 2.24\). Sensitivity check: initialize instead with \(\boldsymbol\mu_1^{(0)}=(1,1), \boldsymbol\mu_2^{(0)}=(2,1)\) (both in left cluster). First assignment puts ALL points closer to \(\boldsymbol\mu_2\) except possibly A? Distances from A: to (1,1)=0, to (2,1)=1 → A→cluster1, rest→cluster2. New \(\boldsymbol\mu_1=(1,1)\), \(\boldsymbol\mu_2=\)mean of \(\{B,C,D,E,F\}\)=(3.8,3.8). Next iteration likely splits differently. Poor initialization can trap in bad local minima where natural clusters aren't recovered. This motivates k-means++ below.

k-Means++ initialization

Lloyd's algorithm is sensitive to initialization. Random initialization can place multiple centers in the same true cluster, leaving others unrepresented and converging to poor solutions. k-means++ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007) provides a probabilistic initialization with theoretical guarantees:

  1. Choose \(\boldsymbol\mu_1\) uniformly at random from the data.
  2. For \(j = 2, \dots, k\): compute \(D(\mathbf{x}_i) = \min_{\ell < j} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol\mu_\ell\|^2\) (distance to nearest existing center). Choose \(\boldsymbol\mu_j\) as \(\mathbf{x}_i\) with probability proportional to \(D(\mathbf{x}_i)^2\).

This spreads initial centers across the data space, making it unlikely that two centers start in the same dense region. The expected value of the resulting k-means objective is \(O(\log k)\)-competitive with the global optimum — exponentially better than random initialization's worst case. In practice, k-means++ followed by Lloyd's algorithm reliably finds high-quality solutions with few restarts.

Edge case: when data contains duplicate points or very tight clusters, \(D(\mathbf{x}_i)\) can be zero for many points, causing numerical issues in probability computation. Add a small epsilon to denominators or handle zero-distance points specially. Also note that k-means++ adds \(O(nk)\) preprocessing cost, which is negligible compared to Lloyd's iterations for large \(n\) but noticeable for tiny datasets. For streaming or distributed settings, scalable variants (k-means||) parallelize the seeding phase.

Worked example: k-means++ seeding probabilities

Same six-point dataset. Step 1: suppose \(\boldsymbol\mu_1 = A = (1,1)\) chosen randomly. Compute squared distances to \(\boldsymbol\mu_1\):

\[ D^2 = [0, 1, 1, 32, 41, 41], \quad \text{sum} = 115 \]

Probabilities for choosing \(\boldsymbol\mu_2\): \(P(A)=0, P(B)=1/115, P(C)=1/115, P(D)=32/115 \approx 0.278, P(E)=41/115 \approx 0.357, P(F)=41/115 \approx 0.357\). Points D, E, F (right cluster) collectively have probability \(114/115 \approx 0.991\) of being selected as \(\boldsymbol\mu_2\), ensuring the two natural clusters are represented from the start. Contrast with uniform random selection: probability of picking both centers from left cluster = \(3/6 \times 2/5 = 0.2\), leading to suboptimal initialization 20% of the time. Sensitivity check: if point D were moved to (2,2), its \(D^2\) would drop to 2, reducing right-cluster selection probability to \((2+41+41)/86 \approx 0.977\) — still high but less certain. As clusters become less separated, k-means++ advantage diminishes because any initialization becomes ambiguous. The method shines precisely when clusters are well-separated but unknown a priori.

k-Medoids: robustness and non-Euclidean distances

K-means uses cluster means, which are sensitive to outliers (a single extreme point pulls the mean toward it) and undefined for non-Euclidean data (e.g., strings, graphs, categorical variables). k-medoids replaces means with medoids: actual data points that minimize total dissimilarity within each cluster. The objective is:

\[ J_{\text{medoid}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{k} r_{ij} \, d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{m}_j) \]

where \(d\) is any dissimilarity measure (not necessarily Euclidean) and \(\mathbf{m}_j \in \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\}\) is constrained to be an observed point. The PAM (Partitioning Around Medoids) algorithm alternates:

  1. Assignment: assign each point to nearest medoid under \(d\).
  2. Update: for each cluster, try swapping the current medoid with every non-medoid point; keep the swap that maximally decreases \(J\). Repeat until no improving swap exists.

PAM is computationally expensive (\(O(n^2 k)\) per iteration vs. \(O(nkp)\) for k-means) but handles arbitrary dissimilarities and is robust to outliers since medoids are always observed points. CLARA and CLARANS scale PAM to larger datasets via sampling.

Common mistake: using k-medoids when k-means suffices. If data is Euclidean and roughly spherical, k-means is faster and often equally effective. Reserve k-medoids for non-Euclidean data, ordinal/categorical features, or when outlier robustness is critical. Also note that k-medoids optimizes a different objective than k-means; comparing their \(J\) values directly is meaningless since they measure different quantities.

Worked example: k-medoids vs. k-means with outlier

Data: five points on \(\mathbb{R}\): \(\{1, 2, 3, 4, 100\}\). True structure: one cluster around 1–4, one outlier at 100. Set \(k=2\).

K-means: Optimal partition minimizes within-cluster SS. Putting 100 alone: cluster1=\(\{1,2,3,4\}\), mean=2.5, SS=5; cluster2=\(\{100\}\), SS=0; total \(J=5\). Alternative: cluster1=\(\{1,2,3\}\), mean=2, SS=2; cluster2=\(\{4,100\}\), mean=52, SS=4608; total \(J=4610\). K-means correctly isolates the outlier. But if we add another outlier at 99: \(\{1,2,3,4,99,100\}\). Now optimal might be \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) (SS=5) and \(\{99,100\}\) (mean=99.5, SS=0.5), \(J=5.5\). Means are (2.5, 99.5) — reasonable. But if outliers were at 99 and -95: \(\{1,2,3,4,-95,99\}\). Optimal split might put negatives together: \(\{-95\}\) and rest, or \(\{-95,1,2,3,4\}\) mean=-17, SS=... Actually k-means struggles when outliers pull means away from dense regions. With \(\{-95,1,2,3,4,99\}\), putting \(\{-95\}\) alone (SS=0) and rest (mean=21.8, SS≈5800) vs. splitting \(\{-95,1,2\}\) (mean=-30.67, SS≈6500) and \(\{3,4,99\}\) (mean=35.33, SS≈4600) — worse. So k-means still isolates -95. The real failure mode is when outliers are numerous enough to form their own cluster, merging with nearby legitimate points.

K-medoids: Medoids must be observed points. For \(\{1,2,3,4,100\}\), optimal medoids are likely 2 (or 3) and 100. Assignments: \(\{1,2,3,4\}\) to medoid 2 (total dissimilarity = |1-2|+|2-2|+|3-2|+|4-2|=1+0+1+2=4); \(\{100\}\) to medoid 100 (dissimilarity 0). Total \(J=4\). Outlier doesn't distort the representative because medoid 2 is unaffected by 100's magnitude. Sensitivity check: replace 100 with 1000. K-means mean shifts to accommodate; k-medoids medoid stays at 2. Robustness confirmed. For non-Euclidean data (e.g., edit distance between strings), k-medoids works naturally while k-means is undefined.

Choosing k and evaluating clusters

Selecting \(k\) is the central challenge in clustering. Common heuristics:

  • Elbow method: plot \(J(k)\) vs. \(k\); choose \(k\) where marginal decrease sharply diminishes ("elbow"). Subjective and often ambiguous.
  • Silhouette score: for each point, compute \(a(i)\) = avg intra-cluster distance, \(b(i)\) = avg nearest-cluster distance; silhouette \(s(i) = (b(i)-a(i))/\max(a(i),b(i)) \in [-1,1]\). Average silhouette over all points measures cohesion/separation balance. Maximize over \(k\).
  • Gap statistic: compare \(J(k)\) to expected \(J\) under null reference distribution (uniform data). Choose \(k\) maximizing the gap. More principled but computationally intensive.
  • Domain validation: evaluate cluster utility for downstream tasks (e.g., classification accuracy using cluster labels as features, business metric improvement). Gold standard when available.

Edge case: all internal metrics assume specific cluster shapes (spherical for silhouette, convex for gap). When true clusters are irregular (manifold-shaped, varying density), these metrics mislead. Density-based methods (DBSCAN) or spectral clustering may be more appropriate. Also note that \(k\) may not exist as a single correct answer; hierarchical clustering (Chapter 16) reveals structure at multiple resolutions simultaneously.

Connection to the broader framework

Clustering bridges supervised and unsupervised learning throughout this book. Chapter 1's Bayes-optimal predictor inspires generative clustering models (Gaussian mixture models, not covered here but related to LDA in Chapter 8). Chapter 6's kNN shares the nearest-centroid assignment logic but uses labeled neighbors rather than learned centers. Chapter 11's bias-variance trade-off manifests as under-clustering (high bias, merged groups) vs. over-clustering (high variance, fragmented groups). Chapter 17's PCA is often applied before k-means to reduce dimensionality and mitigate the curse of dimensionality (Chapter 6). And the entire supervised curriculum contrasts with clustering's fundamental ambiguity: without labels, evaluation is inherently subjective and application-dependent.

Master k-means and k-medoids not as universal solutions but as foundational tools whose limitations define the landscape of clustering research. Understand why k-means assumes spherical clusters, why initialization matters profoundly, why k-medoids trades speed for robustness, and why no internal metric substitutes for domain validation. If you can derive Lloyd's algorithm from coordinate descent, implement k-means++ seeding by hand, explain when k-medoids beats k-means, and diagnose inappropriate \(k\) from silhouette plots, you have internalized the optimization-based paradigm that anchors all subsequent unsupervised methods.
