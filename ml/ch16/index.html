16. Hierarchical Clustering

Chapter 16 of 17 24 min read

Beyond flat partitions: the hierarchical view

Chapter 15’s k-means produces a single flat partition at a pre-specified \(k\). But many datasets possess structure at multiple scales: genes group into pathways that group into functional modules; documents cluster by topic within subtopics within broader domains; customers segment by behavior within demographics within regions. Hierarchical clustering captures this multi-resolution structure by producing a dendrogram — a tree whose leaves are individual data points and whose internal nodes represent merges (or splits) at increasing dissimilarity levels. Cutting the dendrogram at any height yields a valid flat partition, enabling exploration of structure without committing to a single \(k\) upfront.

Two paradigms exist: agglomerative (bottom-up) starts with each point as its own cluster and iteratively merges the closest pair; divisive (top-down) starts with all points in one cluster and recursively splits. Agglomerative methods dominate practice due to computational simplicity and well-understood linkage criteria. This chapter focuses on agglomerative clustering with detailed treatment of linkage functions, the Lance-Williams recurrence that unifies them, dendrogram interpretation, and comparisons across linkage choices via hand-computed examples.

Common mistake: treating the dendrogram as a definitive representation of "true" hierarchy. The tree is an artifact of the chosen linkage criterion and distance metric; different linkages produce structurally different trees from identical data. Single linkage chains elongated clusters; complete linkage fragments them; average linkage compromises. Always interpret dendrograms relative to the linkage used and validate cuts against domain knowledge or stability analysis rather than assuming the tree reveals ground truth.

Agglomerative algorithm and linkage criteria

Agglomerative hierarchical clustering proceeds as follows:

  1. Initialize \(n\) singleton clusters \(\{x_1\}, \dots, \{x_n\}\).
  2. Compute pairwise distances between all clusters using a linkage function \(d(C_i, C_j)\).
  3. Merge the two clusters with smallest \(d(C_i, C_j)\). Record the merge height as \(d(C_i, C_j)\).
  4. Update distances between the new cluster and all remaining clusters.
  5. Repeat steps 2–4 until one cluster remains.

The choice of linkage function defines the algorithm's behavior. Four standard linkages exist for Euclidean data:

Single linkage (minimum)

\[ d_{\text{single}}(C_i, C_j) = \min_{x \in C_i, y \in C_j} \|x - y\| \]

Distance between closest points. Produces elongated, chain-like clusters. Sensitive to noise bridges connecting otherwise separate groups. Equivalent to constructing a minimum spanning tree and cutting edges above threshold.

Complete linkage (maximum)

\[ d_{\text{complete}}(C_i, C_j) = \max_{x \in C_i, y \in C_j} \|x - y\| \]

Distance between farthest points. Produces compact, spherical clusters of similar diameter. Sensitive to outliers that inflate maximum distances. Guarantees that all points within a merged cluster are within \(d\) of each other.

Average linkage (UPGMA)

\[ d_{\text{avg}}(C_i, C_j) = \frac{1}{|C_i||C_j|} \sum_{x \in C_i} \sum_{y \in C_j} \|x - y\| \]

Mean pairwise distance. Balances single and complete linkage tendencies. Less sensitive to outliers than complete, less prone to chaining than single. Most widely recommended default.

Ward’s linkage

\[ d_{\text{Ward}}(C_i, C_j) = \frac{|C_i||C_j|}{|C_i| + |C_j|} \|\boldsymbol\mu_i - \boldsymbol\mu_j\|^2 \]

Increase in within-cluster sum of squares upon merging. Minimizes variance inflation at each step. Tends to produce equal-sized, spherical clusters analogous to k-means but hierarchically. Only defined for Euclidean distance.

Edge case: ties in minimum distance can occur frequently with discrete or quantized data. Deterministic tie-breaking (e.g., by index order) produces reproducible but potentially arbitrary trees; randomized tie-breaking reveals instability. When many ties exist, the dendrogram topology may not be meaningful — consider whether the distance metric adequately captures similarity.

Worked example: comparing linkages on four points

Data: \(A=(0,0), B=(1,0), C=(4,0), D=(5,0)\) on \(\mathbb{R}\). Natural structure: two pairs \(\{A,B\}\) and \(\{C,D\}\) separated by gap of 3.

Pairwise distances: \(d(A,B)=1, d(C,D)=1, d(B,C)=3, d(A,C)=4, d(B,D)=4, d(A,D)=5\).

Step 1 (all linkages): Minimum distance is 1, achieved by both \(\{A,B\}\) and \(\{C,D\}\). Merge both simultaneously (or sequentially; result identical). Now clusters: \(\{AB\}, \{CD\}\).

Step 2 distances between \(\{AB\}\) and \(\{CD\}\):

  • Single: \(\min(d(A,C), d(A,D), d(B,C), d(B,D)) = \min(4,5,3,4) = 3\)
  • Complete: \(\max(4,5,3,4) = 5\)
  • Average: \((4+5+3+4)/4 = 4\)
  • Ward: \(\frac{2 \cdot 2}{2+2} \|(0.5,0)-(4.5,0)\|^2 = 1 \cdot 16 = 16\)

All linkages merge \(\{AB\}\) and \(\{CD\}\) next (only two clusters remain), but at very different heights: single=3, average=4, complete=5, Ward=16. Dendrogram interpretation: Single linkage shows a long branch at height 3 separating the two natural clusters — clear signal. Complete linkage shows separation at height 5, still clear but compressed scale. Average linkage intermediate. Ward’s large merge height reflects substantial variance increase when combining distant groups. Sensitivity check: add noise point \(E=(2.5, 0)\) bridging the gap. Single linkage now chains: merges \(E\) with \(B\) at distance 1.5, then \(\{BE\}\) with \(A\) at 2.5, then \(\{ABE\}\) with \(C\) at 1.5 — destroying the two-cluster structure entirely. Complete linkage: \(E\) merges with nearest neighbor first, but final merge still occurs at high distance preserving separation. Average linkage partially affected. This demonstrates single linkage’s notorious chaining vulnerability versus complete/average robustness.

Lance-Williams recurrence: unified distance updates

Naively recomputing all pairwise distances after each merge costs \(O(n^3)\) total. The Lance-Williams recurrence enables efficient \(O(n^2)\) implementation by expressing updated distances as linear combinations of previous distances. When clusters \(C_i\) and \(C_j\) merge to form \(C_r = C_i \cup C_j\), the distance to any other cluster \(C_k\) is:

\[ d(C_r, C_k) = \alpha_i d(C_i, C_k) + \alpha_j d(C_j, C_k) + \beta d(C_i, C_j) + \gamma |d(C_i, C_k) - d(C_j, C_k)| \]

Coefficients depend only on cluster sizes \(|C_i|, |C_j|, |C_k|\) and the linkage choice:

\[ \begin{array}{l|cccc} \text{Linkage} & \alpha_i & \alpha_j & \beta & \gamma \\ \hline \text{Single} & 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 & -1/2 \\ \text{Complete} & 1/2 & 1/2 & 0 & 1/2 \\ \text{Average} & |C_i|/(|C_i|+|C_j|) & |C_j|/(|C_i|+|C_j|) & 0 & 0 \\ \text{Ward} & (|C_i|+|C_k|)/(|C_i|+|C_j|+|C_k|) & (|C_j|+|C_k|)/(|C_i|+|C_j|+|C_k|) & -|C_k|/(|C_i|+|C_j|+|C_k|) & 0 \end{array} \]

This recurrence allows updating only the \(O(n)\) distances involving the new cluster per merge, yielding \(O(n^2)\) total time and \(O(n^2)\) space for the distance matrix. Without it, hierarchical clustering would be impractical beyond tiny datasets.

Edge case: Ward’s coefficients depend on \(|C_k|\), requiring access to all cluster sizes during update — slightly more complex bookkeeping than size-independent linkages. Also note that Lance-Williams assumes the distance matrix fits in memory; for very large \(n\), approximate methods (SLINK, CLINK) or sampling-based approaches are necessary. The recurrence also reveals why certain linkages preserve metric properties: single and complete maintain ultrametricity (merge heights are non-decreasing), while average and Ward guarantee monotonicity under mild conditions.

Worked example: verifying Lance-Williams for average linkage

Using the four-point example above, after merging \(A,B\) into \(AB\) (\(|AB|=2\)) and \(C,D\) into \(CD\) (\(|CD|=2\)), compute \(d(AB, CD)\) via recurrence. For average linkage: \(\alpha_{AB} = |A|/(|A|+|B|) = 1/2\), \(\alpha_{CD} = 1/2\), \(\beta = 0\), \(\gamma = 0\). But wait — the recurrence applies when merging \(C_i, C_j\) into \(C_r\) and computing \(d(C_r, C_k)\). Here we need \(d(AB, CD)\) where both are already merged. Apply recurrence treating \(AB\) as formed from \(A,B\) and querying against \(CD\):

\[ d(AB, CD) = \frac{1}{2} d(A, CD) + \frac{1}{2} d(B, CD) \]

We need \(d(A, CD)\) and \(d(B, CD)\). These were computed when \(CD\) was formed from \(C,D\):

\[ d(A, CD) = \frac{1}{2} d(A,C) + \frac{1}{2} d(A,D) = \frac{1}{2}(4) + \frac{1}{2}(5) = 4.5 \] \[ d(B, CD) = \frac{1}{2} d(B,C) + \frac{1}{2} d(B,D) = \frac{1}{2}(3) + \frac{1}{2}(4) = 3.5 \]

Therefore: \(d(AB, CD) = \frac{1}{2}(4.5) + \frac{1}{2}(3.5) = 4.0\). Matches direct average computation \((4+5+3+4)/4 = 4\) ✓. Sensitivity check: for single linkage, \(\gamma = -1/2\) introduces the absolute difference term: \(d(AB, CD) = \frac{1}{2} d(A,CD) + \frac{1}{2} d(B,CD) - \frac{1}{2}|d(A,CD) - d(B,CD)| = \min(d(A,CD), d(B,CD))\). With \(d(A,CD)=\min(4,5)=4\), \(d(B,CD)=\min(3,4)=3\), gives \(\min(4,3)=3\) ✓. The recurrence correctly encodes min/max operations via the \(\gamma\) term.

Divisive clustering: top-down approaches

Divisive hierarchical clustering starts with all \(n\) points in one cluster and recursively splits. At each step, select a cluster to split and partition it into two subclusters. Key challenges:

  • Split criterion: Unlike agglomerative merging (well-defined via linkage), splitting lacks a canonical objective. Common approaches: maximize between-subcluster distance, minimize within-subcluster variance (analogous to Ward), or use spectral bipartitioning.
  • Cluster selection: Which cluster to split next? Options include largest cluster, highest within-cluster variance, or oldest cluster.
  • Computational cost: Naive divisive algorithms are \(O(2^n)\) without constraints; practical implementations restrict split candidates or use heuristics.

DIANA (DIvisive ANAlysis) is the most known divisive algorithm, using a dissimilarity-based splitting criterion. In practice, divisive methods are rarely used except when top-down structure is semantically meaningful (e.g., taxonomy construction) or when agglomerative chaining is problematic. Most modern applications prefer agglomerative methods with appropriate linkage or density-based alternatives.

Edge case: divisive methods can recover structures that agglomerative methods miss when global structure should guide local splits. For example, if two dense clusters are connected by a thin bridge, agglomerative single linkage chains through the bridge immediately, while divisive methods may first split at the bridge’s midpoint, correctly separating the clusters. However, this advantage comes at significant computational and implementation complexity.

Dendrogram interpretation and cutting strategies

The dendrogram encodes the entire hierarchy. Key interpretive principles:

  • Merge height: vertical position of internal node = dissimilarity at which children merged. Large jumps indicate natural cluster boundaries; gradual increases suggest continuous structure.
  • Branch length: difference between parent and child merge heights. Long branches indicate stable, well-separated clusters; short branches indicate ambiguous assignments.
  • Monotonicity: valid dendrograms have non-decreasing merge heights (ultrametric property). Non-monotonic trees indicate implementation errors or inappropriate linkage/distance combinations.

Cutting strategies to extract flat partitions:

  • Height threshold: cut at fixed dissimilarity level. Natural when domain specifies meaningful similarity scale.
  • Number of clusters: cut to obtain desired \(k\). Equivalent to k-means’ requirement but informed by hierarchy.
  • Largest gap: find biggest jump in merge heights; cut just below it. Heuristic for "natural" \(k\).
  • Dynamic tree cut: adaptive methods that identify branches with consistent shape/size, allowing variable-height cuts across the tree.

Common mistake: interpreting absolute merge heights across different datasets or distance metrics. Heights are meaningful only relative to the specific distance scale used. Normalizing distances before clustering or comparing normalized cophenetic correlations enables cross-dataset comparison. Also beware that dendrogram leaf ordering is arbitrary; rotating subtrees doesn’t change the hierarchy but affects visual perception of cluster proximity.

Worked example: identifying natural clusters from merge heights

Six-point dataset with merge sequence (agglomerative, average linkage):

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} \text{Merge} & \text{Height} \\ \hline \{A,B\} & 0.5 \\ \{C,D\} & 0.6 \\ \{E,F\} & 0.7 \\ \{AB,CD\} & 2.1 \\ \{ABC D, EF\} & 8.5 \end{array} \]

Gaps between successive merges: \(0.6-0.5=0.1\), \(0.7-0.6=0.1\), \(2.1-0.7=1.4\), \(8.5-2.1=6.4\). Largest gap is 6.4 between merges 4 and 5 → suggests \(k=2\) (clusters \(\{ABCD\}\) and \(\{EF\}\)). Second-largest gap is 1.4 → suggests \(k=3\) (clusters \(\{AB\}, \{CD\}, \{EF\}\)). Both are plausible; choice depends on application granularity. Sensitivity check: if last merge height were 3.0 instead of 8.5, gap would shrink to 0.9, making \(k=2\) less compelling. If all gaps were ~0.5, no natural \(k\) exists — data may be continuously structured or poorly represented by the chosen metric. Always report gap magnitudes alongside recommended \(k\) to convey confidence.

Connection to other methods and practical guidance

Hierarchical clustering connects to several themes across this book. Chapter 15’s k-means emerges as a flat approximation to Ward’s hierarchical method; indeed, Ward’s dendrogram cut at \(k\) often approximates k-means solutions. Chapter 6’s kNN shares single linkage’s nearest-neighbor logic but uses labels rather than unsupervised merges. Chapter 17’s PCA provides complementary dimensionality reduction before hierarchical clustering to mitigate curse-of-dimensionality effects on distance metrics. And the bias-variance trade-off (Chapter 11) manifests as under-segmentation (few large clusters, high bias) vs. over-segmentation (many small clusters, high variance) when choosing cut height.

Practical recommendations: Use average linkage as default unless domain knowledge dictates otherwise. Use Ward’s for Euclidean data when compact, equal-sized clusters are expected. Avoid single linkage unless chaining is genuinely desired (e.g., contour detection). Always visualize dendrograms alongside raw data projections. Validate stability via bootstrap resampling or subsampling: if cluster assignments change dramatically across samples, the hierarchy is unreliable. For large \(n > 10^4\), consider approximate methods (BIRCH, CURE) or switch to k-means with multiple restarts.

Edge case: hierarchical clustering assumes transitive similarity: if A~B and B~C, then A~C. This fails for manifold-structured data where geodesic distance differs from Euclidean. Spectral clustering or density-based methods (DBSCAN) handle such cases better. Also note that hierarchical methods don’t natively handle mixed data types; Gower distance extends applicability but requires careful weighting of feature contributions.

Connection to the broader framework

Hierarchical clustering completes the unsupervised learning trilogy alongside k-means (Chapter 15) and PCA (Chapter 17). Where k-means optimizes a global objective for fixed \(k\), hierarchical methods reveal structure at all resolutions without pre-specifying \(k\). Where PCA finds linear projections maximizing variance, hierarchical clustering finds nested partitions optimizing local cohesion/separation. Together they provide complementary lenses: PCA reduces dimensionality to make distances meaningful; k-means finds representative centers; hierarchical clustering exposes multi-scale organization.

Master hierarchical clustering not as a universal tool but as the primary method for exploratory multi-resolution analysis. Understand why linkage choice fundamentally shapes results, how Lance-Williams enables scalability, when divisive beats agglomerative, and how to read dendrograms critically rather than dogmatically. If you can compute merge sequences by hand for different linkages, verify Lance-Williams recurrences, diagnose chaining from dendrogram shape, and justify cut selection via gap analysis, you have internalized the structural perspective that complements the optimization-based view of k-means and the geometric view of PCA.
