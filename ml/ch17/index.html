17. Dimensionality Reduction and Principal Component Analysis

Chapter 17 of 17 26 min read

The curse of dimensionality and the case for reduction

High-dimensional data poses fundamental challenges for nearly every algorithm in this book. Chapter 6 showed that k-nearest neighbours degrades as distances concentrate; Chapter 3 revealed that multicollinearity inflates OLS variance; Chapter 15 demonstrated that k-means requires exponentially more data to maintain density; and Chapters 13–14 highlighted that neural networks need massive samples to constrain their enormous parameter spaces. These are all manifestations of the curse of dimensionality: as ambient dimension \(p\) grows, volume expands exponentially, data become sparse relative to the space they occupy, and statistical estimation breaks down.

Dimensionality reduction addresses this by finding a low-dimensional representation \(\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (\(d \ll p\)) that preserves the essential structure of the original data \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^p\). Two paradigms exist: feature selection retains a subset of original features (interpretable but discrete/combinatorial); feature extraction constructs new features as transformations of the originals (continuous/optimizable but less interpretable). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the canonical feature extraction method: it finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance, providing an optimal linear compression under squared reconstruction error. This chapter derives PCA from two equivalent perspectives (variance maximization and minimum reconstruction error), connects it to SVD, covers whitening and kernel extensions, and provides hand-computed examples throughout.

Common mistake: treating PCA components as semantically meaningful features. Principal components are linear combinations optimized for variance, not interpretability. A component mixing age, income, and education may capture maximal variance but resist causal interpretation. When interpretability matters, prefer factor analysis, sparse PCA, or domain-informed feature engineering. Also beware that PCA assumes linearity; nonlinear manifold structure requires kernel PCA, autoencoders, or manifold learning methods.

PCA via variance maximization: derivation

Given centered data matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) (each column has zero mean), PCA seeks a unit vector \(\mathbf{w}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^p\) maximizing the sample variance of projected data \(X \mathbf{w}_1\):

\[ \max_{\mathbf{w}_1} \frac{1}{n} \| X \mathbf{w}_1 \|^2 = \max_{\mathbf{w}_1} \mathbf{w}_1^\top \left( \frac{X^\top X}{n} \right) \mathbf{w}_1 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \|\mathbf{w}_1\|^2 = 1 \]

The matrix \(S = X^\top X / n\) is the sample covariance matrix. This is a constrained optimization problem solvable via Lagrange multipliers. Define the Lagrangian:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}_1, \lambda) = \mathbf{w}_1^\top S \mathbf{w}_1 - \lambda (\mathbf{w}_1^\top \mathbf{w}_1 - 1) \]

Setting \(\nabla_{\mathbf{w}_1} \mathcal{L} = 0\) yields:

\[ 2 S \mathbf{w}_1 - 2 \lambda \mathbf{w}_1 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad S \mathbf{w}_1 = \lambda \mathbf{w}_1 \]

This is the eigenvalue equation: optimal projection directions are eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and the achieved variance equals the corresponding eigenvalue \(\lambda\). To maximize variance, choose the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue. Subsequent components are found by maximizing variance subject to orthogonality constraints \(\mathbf{w}_j^\top \mathbf{w}_k = 0\) for \(j \neq k\), yielding the remaining eigenvectors in descending eigenvalue order.

The resulting principal components are uncorrelated (orthogonal in both coefficient space and projected data space) and ordered by explained variance. The first \(d\) components capture the maximum possible variance among all rank-\(d\) linear projections — this optimality is the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem applied to the covariance matrix.

Edge case: when multiple eigenvalues are equal, the corresponding eigenspace is degenerate: any orthonormal basis within that subspace achieves the same variance. PCA implementations return arbitrary bases in such cases; results are not unique though the subspace is. Also note that PCA on uncentered data finds directions maximizing second moments rather than variance, conflating location with spread. Always center before applying PCA (and optionally scale to unit variance if features have different units).

Worked example: hand-computed PCA on four points

Data: \(n = 4\) points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): \((1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,4)\) plus noise perturbation to break perfect collinearity: actually use \((1,1.1), (2,1.9), (3,3.2), (4,3.8)\). Center: \(\bar x = 2.5, \bar y = 2.5\). Centered data:

\[ X_c = \begin{pmatrix} -1.5 & -1.4 \\ -0.5 & -0.6 \\ 0.5 & 0.7 \\ 1.5 & 1.3 \end{pmatrix} \]

Covariance matrix \(S = X_c^\top X_c / 4\):

\[ X_c^\top X_c = \begin{pmatrix} 2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25 & 2.1+0.3+0.35+1.95 \\ \cdot & 1.96+0.36+0.49+1.69 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5.0 & 4.7 \\ 4.7 & 4.5 \end{pmatrix} \] \[ S = \begin{pmatrix} 1.25 & 1.175 \\ 1.175 & 1.125 \end{pmatrix} \]

Eigenvalues: solve \(\det(S - \lambda I) = (1.25-\lambda)(1.125-\lambda) - 1.175^2 = \lambda^2 - 2.375\lambda + 1.40625 - 1.380625 = \lambda^2 - 2.375\lambda + 0.025625 = 0\).

\[ \lambda = \frac{2.375 \pm \sqrt{2.375^2 - 4(0.025625)}}{2} = \frac{2.375 \pm \sqrt{5.640625 - 0.1025}}{2} = \frac{2.375 \pm 2.351}{2} \]

\(\lambda_1 \approx 2.363, \lambda_2 \approx 0.012\). First PC explains \(2.363 / (2.363 + 0.012) = 99.5\%\) of variance — data is nearly 1D. Eigenvector for \(\lambda_1\): solve \((S - 2.363 I)\mathbf{w} = 0\):

\[ \begin{pmatrix} -1.113 & 1.175 \\ 1.175 & -1.238 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \Rightarrow w_2 \approx 0.947 w_1 \]

Normalized: \(\mathbf{w}_1 \approx (0.727, 0.687)^\top\) (direction ~43°, close to diagonal as expected). Sensitivity check: if we doubled \(y\)-values to \((1,2.2), (2,3.8), (3,6.4), (4,7.6)\), the covariance would shift dramatically: \(S_{22}\) quadruples while \(S_{12}\) doubles, rotating the first PC toward the \(y\)-axis. PCA adapts to the dominant variance direction regardless of coordinate system — but this also means it's sensitive to scaling, motivating standardized PCA when units differ.

PCA via minimum reconstruction error

An equivalent formulation frames PCA as finding the rank-\(d\) approximation minimizing squared reconstruction error. For projection onto \(d\) orthonormal vectors \(W_d = [\mathbf{w}_1, \dots, \mathbf{w}_d]\), the reconstructed data is \(\hat X = X W_d W_d^\top\). The reconstruction error is:

\[ \| X - \hat X \|_F^2 = \| X \|_F^2 - \| X W_d \|_F^2 = \sum_{j=d+1}^{p} \lambda_j \]

where \(\lambda_1 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_p\) are eigenvalues of \(S\). Minimizing reconstruction error is therefore equivalent to maximizing retained variance \(\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j\). This duality reveals PCA as the optimal linear compressor under squared loss: no other rank-\(d\) linear map achieves lower reconstruction error.

The connection to SVD makes computation efficient. The thin SVD of centered data is \(X_c = U \Sigma V^\top\), where \(V\) contains eigenvectors of \(S\) and \(\Sigma^2/n\) gives eigenvalues. Projected coordinates are \(Z = X_c V_d = U_d \Sigma_d\), avoiding explicit covariance formation (important when \(p \gg n\)). Reconstruction is \(\hat X_c = Z V_d^\top = U_d \Sigma_d V_d^\top\), the best rank-\(d\) approximation by Eckart-Young.

Edge case: when \(n < p\), the sample covariance has rank at most \(n-1\), so at most \(n-1\) nonzero eigenvalues exist. Computing the full \(p \times p\) eigendecomposition is wasteful; instead compute the \(n \times n\) Gram matrix \(X_c X_c^\top / n\) whose nonzero eigenvalues match those of \(S\), then recover eigenvectors via \(V = X_c^\top U \Sigma^{-1}\). This "dual PCA" trick reduces complexity from \(O(p^3)\) to \(O(n^3)\) when \(n \ll p\), enabling PCA on high-dimensional data like images or genomics.

Worked example: reconstruction error calculation

Using the previous example with \(\lambda_1 = 2.363, \lambda_2 = 0.012\). Total variance = 2.375. With \(d = 1\):

\[ \text{Retained variance} = 2.363, \quad \text{Reconstruction error} = 0.012, \quad \text{Explained fraction} = 99.5\% \]

Projecting point \((-1.5, -1.4)\) onto \(\mathbf{w}_1 = (0.727, 0.687)\): score = \(-1.5(0.727) + (-1.4)(0.687) = -1.091 - 0.962 = -2.053\). Reconstructed: \(-2.053 \times (0.727, 0.687) = (-1.493, -1.410)\). Original centered: \((-1.5, -1.4)\). Squared error: \((-1.5+1.493)^2 + (-1.4+1.410)^2 = 0.000049 + 0.0001 = 0.000149\). Summing over all four points recovers total error ≈ 0.012 (up to rounding). Sensitivity check: with \(d = 2\) (full rank), reconstruction error = 0 exactly. With \(d = 0\), error = total variance = 2.375. The error decreases monotonically with \(d\), and each additional component reduces error by exactly its eigenvalue — enabling principled selection of \(d\) via cumulative explained variance thresholds (e.g., 95%) or scree plot elbows.

Choosing the number of components

Selecting \(d\) balances compression against information loss. Common strategies:

  • Cumulative explained variance: choose smallest \(d\) such that \(\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j / \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j \geq \tau\) (typically \(\tau = 0.90\) or \(0.95\)). Simple and widely used but arbitrary threshold.
  • Scree plot: plot \(\lambda_j\) vs. \(j\); look for an "elbow" where eigenvalues transition from steep decline to flat tail. Subjective but visually informative about intrinsic dimensionality.
  • Kaiser criterion: retain components with \(\lambda_j > 1\) (for correlation matrices) or above average eigenvalue. Often retains too many components; better suited to factor analysis.
  • Cross-validation: treat PCA as preprocessing for downstream task (classification, regression); select \(d\) maximizing validation performance. Gold standard when downstream objective exists.
  • Random matrix theory: compare eigenvalues to Marchenko-Pastur distribution under null hypothesis of random data; retain only statistically significant components. Principled for noisy high-dimensional data.

Common mistake: selecting \(d\) solely based on explained variance without considering downstream utility. 95% variance retention may include noise-dominated components irrelevant to prediction, or exclude low-variance components carrying critical discriminative signal. Always validate against task-specific metrics when possible. Also beware that explained variance is meaningless for non-centered or improperly scaled data — always verify preprocessing before interpreting eigenvalues.

Whitening and probabilistic PCA

Whitening transforms PCA scores to have unit variance and zero correlation: \(\tilde Z = Z \Lambda_d^{-1/2} = U_d\). Whitened features are spherically distributed, removing directional bias and improving conditioning for downstream algorithms (especially kNN, SVMs, and neural networks). However, whitening amplifies noise in low-variance directions since division by small \(\sqrt{\lambda_j}\) inflates those components. Truncated whitening (using only top \(d\) components) mitigates this.

Probabilistic PCA (PPCA) extends PCA with a generative model: \(\mathbf{x} = W \mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\epsilon\), where \(\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\) and \(\boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_p)\). Maximum likelihood estimation recovers PCA directions when \(\sigma^2 \to 0\), but PPCA additionally estimates noise variance \(\sigma^2\), enables Bayesian inference, handles missing data naturally, and provides a principled likelihood-based criterion for selecting \(d\) (BIC/AIC). PPCA bridges PCA to the probabilistic modeling framework of Chapters 7–8.

Edge case: when true data lies on a lower-dimensional manifold corrupted by isotropic noise, PPCA correctly separates signal from noise via \(\sigma^2\), while standard PCA conflates them. However, PPCA assumes Gaussian noise; heavy-tailed or structured noise violates this assumption. Robust PCA variants decompose data into low-rank plus sparse components, handling outliers and gross corruptions that defeat both standard and probabilistic PCA.

Kernel PCA: nonlinear dimensionality reduction

Standard PCA captures only linear structure. Kernel PCA applies the kernel trick (Chapter 9) to perform PCA implicitly in a high-dimensional feature space \(\mathcal{F}\) defined by kernel \(K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \langle \phi(\mathbf{x}), \phi(\mathbf{x}') \rangle\). The key insight is that PCA in \(\mathcal{F}\) depends only on inner products between mapped data points, which the kernel provides without explicit mapping.

The algorithm operates on the centered kernel matrix \(\tilde K\) (centering in feature space requires adjusting \(K\) analytically). Eigendecomposition \(\tilde K \boldsymbol\alpha_j = n \lambda_j \boldsymbol\alpha_j\) yields coefficients \(\boldsymbol\alpha_j\); projected coordinates for training point \(\mathbf{x}_i\) are \(z_{ij} = \sum_k \alpha_{kj} \tilde K(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{x}_i)\). New test points project via \(z_j(\mathbf{x}_{\text{new}}) = \sum_k \alpha_{kj} \tilde K(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{x}_{\text{new}})\).

Kernel PCA captures nonlinear manifold structure inaccessible to linear PCA: RBF kernels unfold curved manifolds, polynomial kernels capture algebraic varieties. However, it inherits kernel SVM's computational cost (\(O(n^3)\) eigendecomposition, \(O(n)\) per test projection) and lacks explicit inverse mapping (reconstructing \(\mathbf{x}\) from \(\mathbf{z}\) requires approximate pre-image methods).

Edge case: kernel PCA's effectiveness depends critically on kernel choice and bandwidth. RBF kernel with inappropriate \(\gamma\) either collapses all points together (too small) or isolates them (too large), producing degenerate spectra. Bandwidth selection via median heuristic or cross-validation is essential. Also note that kernel PCA doesn't guarantee monotonic reconstruction error decrease with added components in input space (only in feature space), complicating \(d\) selection.

Worked example: kernel PCA vs. linear PCA on circular data

Data: 100 points uniformly sampled on unit circle in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with small Gaussian noise. True intrinsic dimensionality = 1 (angle parameterizes the manifold).

Linear PCA: Covariance is approximately isotropic (\(\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2 \approx 0.5\)) because the circle is symmetric. First PC explains ~50% variance; projection onto it collapses opposite sides of the circle together, destroying topology. No linear projection preserves the circular structure.

Kernel PCA (RBF, \(\gamma = 1\)): Kernel matrix captures geodesic proximity along the circle rather than Euclidean chord distance. First kernel PC unfolds the circle into a 1D representation ordered by angle; second PC captures radial variation/noise. Cumulative explained variance reaches 95% at \(d = 2\) vs. requiring \(d = 2\) for only 100% in linear PCA (trivially). More importantly, the 1D kernel PC embedding preserves neighborhood relationships that linear PCA destroys. Sensitivity check: with \(\gamma = 0.01\), kernel becomes nearly constant; spectrum collapses to one dominant eigenvalue with all others near zero — no useful structure extracted. With \(\gamma = 100\), kernel matrix approaches identity; spectrum flattens; each point becomes its own component. Optimal \(\gamma\) lies between these extremes, typically near inverse median pairwise distance. This sensitivity mirrors SVM's \(\gamma\) tuning (Chapter 9) and reinforces that kernel methods require careful hyperparameter selection.

Connection to the broader framework

PCA synthesizes concepts from across this entire book. Chapter 3's multicollinearity diagnosis uses PCA to identify near-singular directions in \(X^\top X\); ridge regression (Chapter 4) shrinks precisely those directions that PCA identifies as low-variance. Chapter 8's LDA performs supervised dimensionality reduction maximizing class separation rather than total variance; PCA is its unsupervised counterpart. Chapter 9's kernel trick enables nonlinear PCA just as it enables nonlinear SVMs. Chapter 11's bias-variance trade-off governs \(d\) selection: too few components increase bias (lost signal), too many increase variance (retained noise). Chapter 15's k-means benefits from PCA preprocessing to mitigate curse-of-dimensionality effects on distance metrics. And Chapter 16's hierarchical clustering often operates on PCA-reduced data to stabilize distance computations in high dimensions.

More fundamentally, PCA embodies the core unsupervised learning principle: extracting structure without labels by optimizing a well-defined objective (variance/reconstruction error). This contrasts with supervised methods' label-dependent objectives but shares the same mathematical machinery (eigendecomposition, optimization, regularization). Master PCA not merely as a preprocessing step but as the paradigmatic example of how linear algebra, statistics, and optimization converge to reveal latent structure in data. If you can derive PCA from both variance and reconstruction perspectives, compute eigendecompositions by hand, explain why whitening helps downstream models, diagnose inappropriate \(d\) from scree plots, and articulate when kernel PCA beats linear PCA (and vice versa), you have completed the journey from simple linear regression to modern unsupervised representation learning.
