2. Multiple Linear Regression

Chapter 2 of 14 30 min read

From one feature to many

In Chapter 1, we predicted an output \(y\) from a single input \(x\) using a straight line \(\hat{y} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\). But real prediction problems almost never have just one feature. A doctor predicting heart disease risk has age, blood pressure, cholesterol, BMI, smoking status, and dozens more. A real estate model has square footage, number of bedrooms, location, age of building, and school district ratings.

The natural question is: how do we generalize the least-squares idea from Chapter 1 to handle \(p\) input features simultaneously?

A naive approach might be to fit a separate simple linear regression for each feature independently. But this ignores a crucial fact: features are often correlated with each other. Square footage and number of bedrooms move together. If we fit them separately, each model attributes all the shared predictive power to its own feature, leading to double-counting and misleading coefficients. We need a method that fits all features jointly, accounting for their correlations. That method is multiple linear regression.

The model in matrix notation

Suppose we have \(n\) observations and \(p\) features. For observation \(i\), the features are \(x_{i1}, x_{i2}, \ldots, x_{ip}\), and the target is \(y_i\). The multiple linear regression model predicts:

\[\hat{y}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip}\]

This is a hyperplane in \(p\)-dimensional space rather than a line in 2D. Writing out sums like this for every equation quickly becomes unwieldy. Matrix notation compresses everything cleanly.

We collect all \(n\) targets into a column vector \(\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n\):

\[\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{pmatrix}\]

We collect all parameters (including the intercept) into a vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta} \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}\):

\[\boldsymbol{\beta} = \begin{pmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_p \end{pmatrix}\]

And we build the design matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (p+1)}\), where each row is one observation's features preceded by a 1 (to absorb the intercept):

\[X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{pmatrix}\]

The first column of all ones is what makes \(\beta_0\) act as the intercept. Without it, the hyperplane would be forced through the origin. With it, the model can shift up or down freely.

Now the entire set of predictions across all \(n\) observations is simply:

\[\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\boldsymbol{\beta}\]

And the vector of residuals is:

\[\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\]

This compact notation is not just aesthetic convenience — it reveals the geometric structure of the problem, which we will exploit in the derivation.

The least-squares objective, revisited

Just as in Chapter 1, we want to minimize the sum of squared residuals. In matrix notation, the RSS is:

\[\text{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 = (\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top (\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta})\]

where \(\|\cdot\|\) denotes the Euclidean norm. Expanding this quadratic form using the distributive property of matrix multiplication (and noting that \(\mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}^\top \mathbf{a}\) for vectors):

\[\text{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{y} - 2\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top X^\top \mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta}\]

This is a quadratic function of the vector \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), analogous to the scalar quadratic we minimized in Chapter 1. Our goal: find the \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) that minimizes it.

Deriving the normal equations

To minimize RSS, we take the gradient with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and set it to zero. This requires two standard results from matrix calculus:

  • Gradient of a linear form: \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} (\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{a}\) for any constant vector \(\mathbf{a}\).
  • Gradient of a quadratic form: \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} (\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top A \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 2A\boldsymbol{\beta}\) when \(A\) is symmetric.

Note that \(X^\top X\) is always symmetric (since \((X^\top X)^\top = X^\top (X^\top)^\top = X^\top X\)), so the second rule applies directly.

Applying these rules term by term to our expanded RSS:

  • \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} (\mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}\) (constant with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\))
  • \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} (-2\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top X^\top \mathbf{y}) = -2 X^\top \mathbf{y}\)
  • \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} (\boldsymbol{\beta}^\top X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta}) = 2 X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta}\)

Combining:

\[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \text{RSS} = -2 X^\top \mathbf{y} + 2 X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta}\]

Setting the gradient equal to zero and dividing by 2:

\[X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\]

This system of \(p+1\) linear equations in \(p+1\) unknowns is called the normal equations. They are the multivariate generalization of the two scalar equations we solved in Chapter 1.

The closed-form OLS estimator

If the matrix \(X^\top X\) is invertible, we can solve the normal equations directly:

\[\boxed{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}}This is one of the most important formulas in all of statistics and machine learning. Every time you call a linear regression function in any software library, this is (usually) what is being computed internally.

Let's verify that this reduces to the Chapter 1 formulas when \(p = 1\). With one feature, \(X\) has two columns (ones and \(x\)), so \(X^\top X\) is a \(2 \times 2\) matrix. Working through the inversion and multiplication recovers exactly \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\) and \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}\). The matrix formula is a genuine generalization, not a different object.

Geometric interpretation: projection

The algebra gives us a formula, but geometry gives us understanding. What is \(X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) geometrically?

The columns of \(X\) span a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) called the column space of \(X\), denoted \(\text{Col}(X)\). This subspace has dimension at most \(p+1\) (it could be less if columns are linearly dependent). Every possible prediction \(X\boldsymbol{\beta}\) lives in this subspace — it is a linear combination of the columns of \(X\).

The observed target vector \(\mathbf{y}\) generally does not lie in \(\text{Col}(X)\) (unless the data happens to be perfectly linear). So we cannot make the residual \(\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\) exactly zero. The best we can do is find the point in \(\text{Col}(X)\) that is closest to \(\mathbf{y}\) in Euclidean distance.

Minimizing \(\|\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\) is exactly finding this closest point. And the closest point in a subspace to an external vector is the orthogonal projection of that vector onto the subspace. Therefore:

\[\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \text{Proj}_{\text{Col}(X)}(\mathbf{y})\]

The residual vector \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is orthogonal to every column of \(X\), which is precisely what the normal equations express: \(X^\top \mathbf{e} = X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) = \mathbf{0}\). Each component of \(X^\top \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\) says that the residual is orthogonal to one column of \(X\).

This geometric picture explains why OLS works, why the solution is unique (when \(X^\top X\) is invertible), and why the residuals sum to zero when an intercept is included (the residual is orthogonal to the all-ones column).

Worked example

Fitting a plane to four points

Let's compute \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}\) by hand for a tiny dataset with \(n = 4\) observations and \(p = 2\) features:

  • Observation 1: \(x_1 = 1, x_2 = 0, y = 2\)
  • Observation 2: \(x_1 = 0, x_2 = 1, y = 3\)
  • Observation 3: \(x_1 = 1, x_2 = 1, y = 5\)
  • Observation 4: \(x_1 = 2, x_2 = 1, y = 6\)

Step 1: Build \(X\) and \(\mathbf{y}\).

\[X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\]

Step 2: Compute \(X^\top X\).

\[X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\]

Computing each entry individually:

  • \((1,1)\): \(1+1+1+1 = 4\)
  • \((1,2)\): \(1+0+1+2 = 4\)
  • \((1,3)\): \(0+1+1+1 = 3\)
  • \((2,1)\): \(4\) (symmetric)
  • \((2,2)\): \(1+0+1+4 = 6\)
  • \((2,3)\): \(0+0+1+2 = 3\)
  • \((3,1)\): \(3\) (symmetric)
  • \((3,2)\): \(3\) (symmetric)
  • \((3,3)\): \(0+1+1+1 = 3\)
\[X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 & 3 \\ 4 & 6 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\]

Step 3: Compute \(X^\top \mathbf{y}\).

\[X^\top \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2+3+5+6 \\ 2+0+5+12 \\ 0+3+5+6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 16 \\ 19 \\ 14 \end{pmatrix}\]

Step 4: Invert \(X^\top X\).

We compute the inverse of the \(3 \times 3\) matrix. First, the determinant:

\[\det(X^\top X) = 4(6 \cdot 3 - 3 \cdot 3) - 4(4 \cdot 3 - 3 \cdot 3) + 3(4 \cdot 3 - 6 \cdot 3)\] \[= 4(18 - 9) - 4(12 - 9) + 3(12 - 18)\] \[= 4(9) - 4(3) + 3(-6) = 36 - 12 - 18 = 6\]

The determinant is 6 (nonzero), so the inverse exists. Computing the adjugate matrix (cofactor transpose) and dividing by 6:

\[(X^\top X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{pmatrix} 9 & -3 & -6 \\ -3 & 3 & 0 \\ -6 & 0 & 8 \end{pmatrix}\]

(Verification: multiplying this by \(X^\top X\) should give the identity. Checking the \((1,1)\) entry: \(\frac{1}{6}(9 \cdot 4 + (-3) \cdot 4 + (-6) \cdot 3) = \frac{1}{6}(36 - 12 - 18) = \frac{6}{6} = 1\). ✓)

Step 5: Compute \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}\).

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \frac{1}{6} \begin{pmatrix} 9 & -3 & -6 \\ -3 & 3 & 0 \\ -6 & 0 & 8 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 16 \\ 19 \\ 14 \end{pmatrix}\]

Computing each component:

  • \(\hat{\beta}_0 = \frac{1}{6}(9 \cdot 16 + (-3) \cdot 19 + (-6) \cdot 14) = \frac{1}{6}(144 - 57 - 84) = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5\)
  • \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{1}{6}((-3) \cdot 16 + 3 \cdot 19 + 0 \cdot 14) = \frac{1}{6}(-48 + 57 + 0) = \frac{9}{6} = 1.5\)
  • \(\hat{\beta}_2 = \frac{1}{6}((-6) \cdot 16 + 0 \cdot 19 + 8 \cdot 14) = \frac{1}{6}(-96 + 0 + 112) = \frac{16}{6} \approx 2.667\)

Step 6: State the fitted model.

\[\hat{y} = 0.5 + 1.5 x_1 + 2.667 x_2\]

This plane is the unique least-squares fit to our four data points. Holding \(x_2\) fixed, increasing \(x_1\) by one unit increases the prediction by 1.5. Holding \(x_1\) fixed, increasing \(x_2\) by one unit increases the prediction by approximately 2.667. The intercept 0.5 is the predicted value when both features are zero.

When does the formula break? Singularity and multicollinearity

The closed-form solution \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}\) requires \(X^\top X\) to be invertible. When is it not?

\(X^\top X\) is singular if and only if the columns of \(X\) are linearly dependent. This happens when:

  • Perfect multicollinearity: One feature is an exact linear combination of others (e.g., including both temperature in Celsius and temperature in Fahrenheit).
  • \(p + 1 > n\): More parameters than observations. The column space cannot have dimension greater than \(n\), so with \(p+1 > n\) columns, dependence is guaranteed.
  • Dummy variable trap: Including indicator variables for all categories of a categorical variable along with an intercept (the indicators sum to the intercept column).

When \(X^\top X\) is singular, there are infinitely many \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) vectors that achieve the same minimum RSS. The OLS solution is not unique. Software typically handles this by dropping redundant columns or using a pseudoinverse, but the underlying issue is real: the data does not contain enough information to distinguish the effects of collinear features.

Near-singularity (high but not perfect correlation among features) is even more insidious. The inverse exists but has very large entries, making \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) extremely sensitive to small changes in \(\mathbf{y}\). Coefficients can have huge magnitudes and wrong signs. This is the motivation for ridge regression, which we cover in Chapter 3: adding a penalty term \(\lambda \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\) to the objective makes \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) always invertible and stabilizes the estimates.

Common mistake

Common mistake: Students often interpret individual coefficients \(\hat{\beta}_j\) as "the effect of feature \(j\) on \(y\)" without qualification. This is only correct ceteris paribus — holding all other features fixed. In observational data where features are correlated, changing \(x_j\) in reality also changes other features, so \(\hat{\beta}_j\) does not represent the total causal effect of \(x_j\). It represents the partial effect conditional on the other features in the model. Omitting a relevant correlated feature changes every coefficient. This is why feature selection and domain knowledge matter as much as the fitting algorithm itself.

Edge case

What happens as \(n \to \infty\) with fixed \(p\)? Under standard regularity conditions (features have finite second moments, no perfect multicollinearity in the population), the law of large numbers ensures that \(\frac{1}{n} X^\top X \to \mathbb{E}[XX^\top]\) and \(\frac{1}{n} X^\top \mathbf{y} \to \mathbb{E}[XY]\). If \(\mathbb{E}[XX^\top]\) is invertible, then \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \to (\mathbb{E}[XX^\top])^{-1} \mathbb{E}[XY]\), which is the population OLS parameter. The estimator is consistent. But if the population covariance is singular (true multicollinearity), consistency fails regardless of sample size — more data cannot fix a fundamentally unidentifiable model.

Where we go next

We now have a complete theory for fitting linear models with many features. But the closed-form OLS estimator has a vulnerability: when features are correlated or numerous, the coefficients become unstable. Chapter 3 introduces ridge regression, which modifies the least-squares objective with an \(\ell_2\) penalty to shrink coefficients toward zero, trading a small amount of bias for a large reduction in variance. The derivation follows the same Socratic pattern, and the resulting formula \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}\) is a direct modification of the normal equations derived here.
