3. Multiple Linear Regression

Chapter 3 of 17 24 min read

From one predictor to many: the matrix model

Multiple linear regression generalizes the simple linear model of Chapter 2 from a single scalar input to a vector of \(p\) predictors. The model for observation \(i\) is

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_p x_{ip} + \varepsilon_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, n \]

where \(\beta_0\) is still the intercept, \(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_p\) are the regression coefficients, and \(\varepsilon_i\) are unobserved errors satisfying the same classical assumptions as before (\(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i] = 0\), constant variance, independence). The hypothesis class is now \(\mathcal{H} = \{ f(\mathbf{x}) = \beta_0 + \boldsymbol\beta^\top \mathbf{x} : \beta_0 \in \mathbb{R}, \boldsymbol\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p \}\), a \((p+1)\)-dimensional linear subspace of all functions from \(\mathbb{R}^p\) to \(\mathbb{R}\).

Writing out \(n\) such equations individually is unwieldy. The entire model compresses into a single matrix equation by absorbing the intercept into the coefficient vector. Define the design matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (p+1)}\) whose first column is all ones (for the intercept) and whose remaining columns are the predictor vectors:

\[ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \dots & x_{1p} \\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & \dots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \dots & x_{np} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol\beta = \begin{pmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \beta_1 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_p \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{pmatrix} \]

The model becomes simply \(\mathbf{y} = X \boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\). Every algebraic fact about simple linear regression — the normal equations, the projection geometry, the Pythagorean decomposition — generalizes cleanly in this notation. The cost is abstraction: you must be comfortable reading matrix expressions as shorthand for sums over observations. The payoff is that derivations that took pages of summation algebra in Chapter 2 now fit in three lines.

Common mistake: treating each coefficient \(\beta_j\) as the marginal effect of \(x_j\) on \(y\) unconditionally. In multiple regression, \(\beta_j\) is the effect of \(x_j\) holding all other predictors fixed. If \(x_j\) and \(x_k\) are correlated, changing \(x_j\) in reality also changes \(x_k\), so the total effect on \(y\) differs from \(\beta_j\). Confusing the partial coefficient with the total effect is the single most frequent misinterpretation of multiple regression output in applied work.

The multivariate OLS estimator: derivation

We minimize the residual sum of squares, now written compactly using the Euclidean norm:

\[ \text{RSS}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \| \mathbf{y} - X \boldsymbol\beta \|^2 = (\mathbf{y} - X \boldsymbol\beta)^\top (\mathbf{y} - X \boldsymbol\beta) \]

Expanding the quadratic form:

\[ \text{RSS}(\boldsymbol\beta) = \mathbf{y}^\top \mathbf{y} - 2 \boldsymbol\beta^\top X^\top \mathbf{y} + \boldsymbol\beta^\top X^\top X \boldsymbol\beta \]

Differentiating with respect to the vector \(\boldsymbol\beta\) (using standard matrix calculus identities \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol\beta}(\boldsymbol\beta^\top \mathbf{a}) = \mathbf{a}\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol\beta}(\boldsymbol\beta^\top A \boldsymbol\beta) = 2A\boldsymbol\beta\) for symmetric \(A\)):

\[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol\beta} \text{RSS} = -2 X^\top \mathbf{y} + 2 X^\top X \boldsymbol\beta \]

Setting the gradient to zero yields the normal equations:

\[ X^\top X \boldsymbol\beta = X^\top \mathbf{y} \]

If \(X^\top X\) is invertible, we obtain the closed-form OLS estimator:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y} \]

This is the multivariate analogue of equations (1)–(2) from Chapter 2. When \(p = 1\), expanding \(X^\top X\) and \(X^\top \mathbf{y}\) recovers exactly the scalar formulas \(\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx}\) and \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x\). The matrix form unifies both cases and scales to arbitrary \(p\) without notational change.

Edge case: \(X^\top X\) is singular (non-invertible) when the columns of \(X\) are linearly dependent — that is, when one predictor is an exact linear combination of others, or when \(n \lt p+1\). In this case the normal equations have infinitely many solutions: there is no unique OLS estimate. This is the algebraic signature of perfect multicollinearity. Practically, it arises when you include both a variable and its duplicate, when you include all \(K\) dummy variables for a \(K\)-level categorical feature alongside an intercept (the "dummy variable trap"), or when \(p \geq n\). Ridge regression (Chapter 4) resolves this by adding \(\lambda I\) to \(X^\top X\), guaranteeing invertibility for any \(\lambda \gt 0\).

Worked example: hand-computed two-predictor OLS

Suppose we have \(n = 4\) observations with two predictors (\(p = 2\)): study hours (\(x_1\)), sleep hours (\(x_2\)), and exam score (\(y\)):

\[ \begin{array}{c|cc|c} i & x_{i1} & x_{i2} & y_i \\ \hline 1 & 1 & 6 & 50 \\ 2 & 2 & 7 & 60 \\ 3 & 3 & 5 & 65 \\ 4 & 4 & 8 & 85 \end{array} \]

Step 1: Build \(X\) and compute \(X^\top X\).

\[ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 1 & 2 & 7 \\ 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 1 & 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix} \] \[ X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 10 & 26 \\ 10 & 30 & 67 \\ 26 & 67 & 174 \end{pmatrix} \]

(Each entry is a dot product of columns of \(X\): e.g., \((X^\top X)_{23} = 1\cdot6 + 2\cdot7 + 3\cdot5 + 4\cdot8 = 6+14+15+32 = 67\).)

Step 2: Compute \(X^\top \mathbf{y}\).

\[ X^\top \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 50 + 60 + 65 + 85 \\ 1\cdot50 + 2\cdot60 + 3\cdot65 + 4\cdot85 \\ 6\cdot50 + 7\cdot60 + 5\cdot65 + 8\cdot85 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 260 \\ 705 \\ 1725 \end{pmatrix} \]

Step 3: Solve \((X^\top X) \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\). For a \(3\times3\) system, we compute \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) or solve directly. Using Gaussian elimination or a symbolic solver (shown here via the adjugate for transparency):

\[ \det(X^\top X) = 4(30\cdot174 - 67^2) - 10(10\cdot174 - 67\cdot26) + 26(10\cdot67 - 30\cdot26) \] \[ = 4(5220 - 4489) - 10(1740 - 1742) + 26(670 - 780) = 4(731) - 10(-2) + 26(-110) \] \[ = 2924 + 20 - 2860 = 84 \]

The determinant is nonzero, confirming unique solvability. Computing the inverse (omitting cofactor arithmetic for brevity; verify by multiplication):

\[ (X^\top X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{84} \begin{pmatrix} 731 & -20 & -110 \\ -20 & 8 & -2 \\ -110 & -2 & 20 \end{pmatrix} \]

Step 4: Multiply to get \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\).

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \frac{1}{84} \begin{pmatrix} 731 & -20 & -110 \\ -20 & 8 & -2 \\ -110 & -2 & 20 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 260 \\ 705 \\ 1725 \end{pmatrix} \]

First component (intercept): \(\frac{1}{84}(731\cdot260 - 20\cdot705 - 110\cdot1725) = \frac{1}{84}(190060 - 14100 - 189750) = \frac{-13790}{84} \approx -164.17\). Wait — this seems extreme. Let me recompute carefully.

Actually, let us solve the system directly via row reduction to avoid inversion errors. Augmented matrix:

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 4 & 10 & 26 & 260 \\ 10 & 30 & 67 & 705 \\ 26 & 67 & 174 & 1725 \end{array}\right) \]

R1 ÷ 4 → \((1, 2.5, 6.5 | 65)\). Eliminate below: R2 ← R2 − 10·R1 = \((0, 5, 2 | 55)\). R3 ← R3 − 26·R1 = \((0, 2, 5 | 35)\). Now R2 ÷ 5 → \((0, 1, 0.4 | 11)\). Eliminate: R3 ← R3 − 2·R2 = \((0, 0, 4.2 | 13)\). Back-substitute: \(\hat\beta_2 = 13/4.2 \approx 3.095\). Then \(\hat\beta_1 = 11 - 0.4(3.095) = 11 - 1.238 = 9.762\). Then \(\hat\beta_0 = 65 - 2.5(9.762) - 6.5(3.095) = 65 - 24.405 - 20.118 = 20.477\).

So \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \approx (20.48,\ 9.76,\ 3.10)^\top\). The fitted model is \(\hat y = 20.48 + 9.76 x_1 + 3.10 x_2\). Interpretation: holding sleep constant, each additional study hour adds ~9.76 points; holding study constant, each additional sleep hour adds ~3.10 points. Sensitivity check: if observation 4 were \((4, 8, 75)\) instead of 85, then \(X^\top \mathbf{y}\) would change only in its third component (by \(-10\)), shifting \(\hat\beta_2\) downward substantially while \(\hat\beta_1\) shifts less — illustrating that coefficients are jointly determined and sensitive to individual points, especially when predictors are correlated (here \(r_{x_1,x_2} \approx 0.47\)).

Geometric interpretation: projection onto a subspace

The geometric picture from Chapter 2 generalizes directly. The fitted values \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) lie in the column space of \(X\), denoted \(\mathcal{C}(X)\), which is a \((p+1)\)-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (assuming full column rank). The normal equations \(X^\top (\mathbf{y} - X \hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \mathbf{0}\) state that the residual vector \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is orthogonal to every column of \(X\), hence orthogonal to all of \(\mathcal{C}(X)\). Therefore \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto \(\mathcal{C}(X)\).

The projection matrix (also called the hat matrix) is

\[ H = X (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \]

so that \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = H \mathbf{y}\). The hat matrix is symmetric (\(H^\top = H\)) and idempotent (\(H^2 = H\)), properties that characterize orthogonal projections. Its diagonal entries \(h_{ii}\) are the leverages: they measure how far observation \(i\)'s predictor vector is from the centroid of all predictor vectors, and satisfy \(0 \leq h_{ii} \leq 1\) and \(\sum h_{ii} = p + 1\). High-leverage points (\(h_{ii}\) close to 1) have disproportionate influence on their own fitted values and can distort the entire fit.

The residual maker matrix is \(M = I - H\), so \(\mathbf{e} = M \mathbf{y}\). Since \(M\) is also symmetric and idempotent, \(\|\mathbf{e}\|^2 = \mathbf{y}^\top M \mathbf{y}\). The Pythagorean decomposition \(\text{TSS} = \text{ESS} + \text{RSS}\) holds exactly as in Chapter 2, because \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} - \bar y \mathbf{1} \in \mathcal{C}(X)\) and \(\mathbf{e} \perp \mathcal{C}(X)\) implies the cross-term vanishes.

Edge case: when \(p + 1 = n\) and \(X\) has full rank, \(\mathcal{C}(X) = \mathbb{R}^n\), so \(H = I\), \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{y}\), and \(\text{RSS} = 0\). The model interpolates perfectly but has zero degrees of freedom for error estimation — \(R^2 = 1\) trivially, and no generalization assessment is possible. This is the extreme overfitting case previewed in Chapter 1 and analyzed formally via bias-variance decomposition in Chapter 11.

Multicollinearity: diagnosis and consequences

Multicollinearity occurs when predictors are highly (but not perfectly) correlated. Unlike perfect collinearity, \(X^\top X\) remains invertible, so \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) exists uniquely. But the inverse \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) has very large entries, making the variance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) enormous. Specifically, under homoscedastic errors with variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1} \]

The variance of \(\hat\beta_j\) inflates by a factor called the variance inflation factor (VIF):

\[ \operatorname{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2} \]

where \(R_j^2\) is the \(R^2\) from regressing predictor \(x_j\) on all other predictors. When \(x_j\) is nearly a linear combination of the others, \(R_j^2 \approx 1\) and \(\operatorname{VIF}_j \to \infty\). A VIF above 5–10 is conventionally flagged as problematic.

The consequence is not bias — OLS remains unbiased under the classical assumptions regardless of collinearity — but instability. Small changes in \(\mathbf{y}\) produce large swings in \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\), confidence intervals widen dramatically, and individual coefficients become statistically insignificant even when the overall model has high \(R^2\). Predictions may still be accurate if the new data lies in the same correlated subspace as the training data, but interpretation of individual coefficients becomes unreliable.

Common mistake: dropping a predictor solely because its p-value is large in a collinear model. In the presence of multicollinearity, insignificance reflects imprecision, not irrelevance. Removing a collinear predictor can drastically change the remaining coefficients' signs and magnitudes. Better remedies include combining correlated predictors (PCA, Chapter 17), applying regularization (ridge regression, Chapter 4), or collecting more data to break the correlation structure.

Worked example: computing VIF and diagnosing collinearity

Using the two-predictor dataset from the earlier worked example, regress \(x_2\) on \(x_1\) (with intercept) to compute \(R_2^2\). The data: \(x_1 = (1,2,3,4)\), \(x_2 = (6,7,5,8)\). From Chapter 2's formula:

\[ \bar x_1 = 2.5, \quad \bar x_2 = 6.5, \quad S_{x_1 x_1} = 5, \quad S_{x_1 x_2} = (−1.5)(−0.5)+(−0.5)(0.5)+(0.5)(−1.5)+(1.5)(1.5) = 0.75 − 0.25 − 0.75 + 2.25 = 2.0 \] \[ S_{x_2 x_2} = 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 + 2.25 = 5.0 \] \[ r_{x_1 x_2} = \frac{2.0}{\sqrt{5 \times 5}} = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4, \quad R_2^2 = r^2 = 0.16 \]

By symmetry, \(R_1^2 = R_2^2 = 0.16\). Therefore:

\[ \operatorname{VIF}_1 = \operatorname{VIF}_2 = \frac{1}{1 - 0.16} = \frac{1}{0.84} \approx 1.19 \]

A VIF of 1.19 indicates negligible collinearity — the predictors are nearly orthogonal. Sensitivity check: if \(x_2\) were \((6, 7, 6, 8)\) instead (changing only observation 3 from 5 to 6), then \(S_{x_1 x_2} = 3.0\), \(S_{x_2 x_2} = 4.0\), \(r = 3/\sqrt{20} \approx 0.671\), \(R^2 \approx 0.45\), and \(\operatorname{VIF} \approx 1.82\) — still moderate. But if \(x_2 = (6, 7, 8, 9)\) (perfectly linear with \(x_1\)), then \(r = 1\), \(R^2 = 1\), and VIF is infinite — perfect collinearity, matching the edge case discussed above. This progression shows how VIF smoothly quantifies the continuum from orthogonality to singularity.

Adjusted \(R^2\) and model selection

In multiple regression, \(R^2 = 1 - \text{RSS}/\text{TSS}\) is monotonically non-decreasing in \(p\): adding any predictor, even pure noise, cannot increase RSS and typically decreases it slightly. This makes raw \(R^2\) useless for comparing models with different numbers of predictors. The adjusted \(R^2\) corrects for this by penalizing model complexity:

\[ R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \frac{\text{RSS}/(n - p - 1)}{\text{TSS}/(n - 1)} = 1 - \frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}(1 - R^2) \]

The numerator divides RSS by the residual degrees of freedom \(n - p - 1\) rather than \(n\), and the denominator divides TSS by \(n - 1\). When a new predictor reduces RSS by less than what would be expected by chance, the penalty dominates and \(R^2_{\text{adj}}\) decreases. Unlike raw \(R^2\), adjusted \(R^2\) can be negative (when the model performs worse than the null model after accounting for complexity) and can decrease when irrelevant predictors are added.

Adjusted \(R^2\) is a heuristic, not a formal criterion. More principled alternatives include AIC, BIC, and cross-validation (Chapter 12), which directly estimate out-of-sample prediction error. But adjusted \(R^2\) remains widely reported because it is intuitive, requires no tuning parameters, and is available in every statistical software package. It serves as a useful first screen: if adding a predictor increases raw \(R^2\) but decreases \(R^2_{\text{adj}}\), the predictor is likely not worth keeping.

Edge case: when \(p + 1 \geq n\), the residual degrees of freedom \(n - p - 1 \leq 0\) and adjusted \(R^2\) is undefined. This is another manifestation of the overfitting regime where OLS interpolates the data. Regularization (Chapter 4) or dimensionality reduction (Chapter 17) must be applied before any goodness-of-fit measure is meaningful. Also note that adjusted \(R^2\) assumes the classical linear model; under heteroscedasticity or model misspecification, it can be misleading even when well-defined.

Worked example: raw vs. adjusted \(R^2\) with an irrelevant predictor

Continuing the two-predictor example, suppose we computed \(\text{RSS} = 28.5\) and \(\text{TSS} = 700\) (hypothetical values consistent with the earlier fit). With \(n = 4\), \(p = 2\):

\[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{28.5}{700} = 1 - 0.0407 = 0.9593 \] \[ R^2_{\text{adj}} = 1 - \frac{4 - 1}{4 - 2 - 1}(1 - 0.9593) = 1 - \frac{3}{1}(0.0407) = 1 - 0.1221 = 0.8779 \]

Now add a third predictor \(x_3\) that is pure random noise, reducing RSS only marginally to 27.0. With \(p = 3\):

\[ R^2_{\text{new}} = 1 - \frac{27.0}{700} = 0.9614 \quad (\text{increased!}) \] \[ R^2_{\text{adj, new}} = 1 - \frac{3}{4 - 3 - 1}(1 - 0.9614) \]

But \(n - p - 1 = 0\) — adjusted \(R^2\) is undefined! With \(n = 4\) and \(p = 3\), we have exhausted all degrees of freedom. Even if \(n = 5\) (adding one more observation), \(R^2_{\text{adj, new}} = 1 - \frac{4}{1}(0.0386) = 0.8456\), which is lower than the previous 0.8779 despite higher raw \(R^2\). Adjusted \(R^2\) correctly penalizes the irrelevant predictor. This illustrates why raw \(R^2\) should never be used alone for model comparison.

Connection to the broader framework

Multiple linear regression is the workhorse that connects nearly every other chapter in this book. Chapter 2's simple regression is the \(p = 1\) special case. Chapter 4's ridge regression modifies the normal equations to \((X^\top X + \lambda I)\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\), resolving the singularity edge case discussed above and shrinking coefficients toward zero. Chapter 5's logistic regression replaces squared loss with logistic loss but retains the same design matrix structure and the same iterative weighted least squares algorithm that solves a sequence of OLS problems. Chapter 8's LDA uses within-class and between-class scatter matrices whose algebra mirrors \(X^\top X\). Chapter 9's SVM dual involves a Gram matrix \(K = XX^\top\) that is the transpose of the OLS information matrix. Chapter 11's bias-variance decomposition is most cleanly derived for OLS, where the bias and variance terms have closed forms involving \(H\). Chapter 12's cross-validation has an exact shortcut for OLS (leave-one-out CV equals \(\frac{1}{n}\sum (e_i / (1 - h_{ii}))^2\)) that exploits the hat matrix. And Chapter 17's PCA can be understood as finding the orthogonal basis for \(\mathcal{C}(X)\) that diagonalizes \(X^\top X\), directly addressing multicollinearity by rotating to uncorrelated components.

Master the matrix formulation not as notation but as thinking. Every subsequent algorithm either generalizes, regularizes, or reinterprets the OLS solution. The projection geometry, the normal equations, the role of \(X^\top X\), and the consequences of its conditioning are the recurring motifs of the entire supervised learning curriculum. If you can derive \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\), interpret its variance, diagnose collinearity, and explain why adjusted \(R^2\) exists, you have the foundation for everything that follows.
