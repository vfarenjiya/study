3. Ridge Regression

Chapter 3 of 14 25 min read

When OLS breaks down

In Chapter 2, we derived the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimator \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}\). This formula works beautifully when \(X^\top X\) is well-conditioned — that is, when its eigenvalues are all reasonably large and similar in magnitude. But what happens when two or more features are nearly identical?

Consider a concrete scenario: you are predicting house prices using both "square footage" and "square meters." These are perfectly collinear (one is exactly 0.0929 times the other). The matrix \(X^\top X\) becomes singular — its determinant is zero, its inverse does not exist, and the OLS formula literally cannot be computed. Even if the correlation is merely 0.999 rather than 1.000, the smallest eigenvalue of \(X^\top X\) becomes tiny, making \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) have enormous entries. The resulting coefficients \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) swing wildly: one feature gets a huge positive coefficient, the other a huge negative coefficient, and they nearly cancel out to produce predictions that are numerically unstable. A tiny change in \(\mathbf{y}\) produces a massive change in \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\).

This is the problem of multicollinearity. The question ridge regression answers is: can we modify the least-squares objective so that the solution is always stable, even when features are highly correlated, while still staying close to the OLS fit?

The naive fix and why it fails

A first instinct might be: just drop one of the correlated features. If square footage and square meters carry the same information, keep only one. This works for perfect collinearity, but it is brittle for near-collinearity. Which feature do you drop? What if three features are pairwise correlated at 0.95 but no pair is redundant on its own? Dropping any single one loses information. You need a method that handles all features simultaneously, shrinking their coefficients in a coordinated way rather than making binary include/exclude decisions.

Another instinct: add a small constant to the diagonal of \(X^\top X\) before inverting. This is actually exactly what ridge regression does — but we need to understand why this corresponds to a principled optimization problem, not just a numerical hack. The justification comes from modifying the objective function itself.

The ridge objective: penalizing large coefficients

Recall that OLS minimizes the residual sum of squares:

\[\text{RSS}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\]

Ridge regression adds a penalty term that discourages large coefficient values:

\[\text{RSS}_{\text{ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \|\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 + \lambda \|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\]

where \(\lambda \geq 0\) is a tuning parameter (hyperparameter) that controls the strength of the penalty, and \(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 = \sum_{j=0}^{p} \beta_j^2\) is the squared \(\ell_2\) norm of the coefficient vector.

Why squared norm rather than absolute value? Two reasons. First, the squared norm is differentiable everywhere (unlike \(|\beta_j|\), which has a corner at zero), so we can find the minimum via calculus just as we did for OLS. Second, the squared norm penalizes many medium-sized coefficients less harshly than one enormous coefficient, encouraging the model to spread predictive weight across correlated features rather than concentrating it in one. This is exactly the behavior we want under multicollinearity.

A critical detail: the intercept \(\beta_0\) is typically excluded from the penalty. Penalizing the intercept would make the solution depend on the arbitrary origin of \(y\)'s measurement scale. In practice, we center \(\mathbf{y}\) and all columns of \(X\) (subtracting their means) before applying ridge, which makes \(\hat{\beta}_0 = 0\) automatically, and then penalize only the remaining \(p\) slope coefficients. For notational simplicity in the derivation below, we assume this centering has been done and write the penalty over all components of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), understanding that in implementation \(\beta_0\) is handled separately.

Deriving the ridge estimator

We minimize \(\text{RSS}_{\text{ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\) by taking the gradient with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) and setting it to zero. Expanding the objective:

\[\text{RSS}_{\text{ridge}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = (\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top (\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}) + \lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}\]

Taking the gradient term by term, using the same matrix calculus rules from Chapter 2:

  • \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} [(\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta})^\top (\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta})] = -2X^\top(\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}) = -2X^\top \mathbf{y} + 2X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta}\)
  • \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} [\lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}^\top \boldsymbol{\beta}] = 2\lambda \boldsymbol{\beta}\)

Combining and setting equal to zero:

\[-2X^\top \mathbf{y} + 2X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta} + 2\lambda \boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}\]

Dividing by 2 and rearranging:

\[(X^\top X + \lambda I)\boldsymbol{\beta} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\]

where \(I\) is the \((p+1) \times (p+1)\) identity matrix. This is the ridge normal equation. Compare it to the OLS normal equation \(X^\top X \boldsymbol{\beta} = X^\top \mathbf{y}\) from Chapter 2: the only difference is the addition of \(\lambda I\) to the left-hand side matrix.

Solving for \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\):

\[\boxed{\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}}This is the ridge regression estimator. It has the same structural form as OLS, but with a modified matrix to invert.

Why the ridge solution always exists

The key question: is \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) always invertible, even when \(X^\top X\) is singular?

Yes, for any \(\lambda > 0\). Here is why. The matrix \(X^\top X\) is always positive semidefinite: for any vector \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{v}^\top (X^\top X) \mathbf{v} = \|X\mathbf{v}\|^2 \geq 0\). Its eigenvalues are therefore all \(\geq 0\). Adding \(\lambda I\) shifts every eigenvalue up by exactly \(\lambda\). So the eigenvalues of \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) are all \(\geq \lambda > 0\). A symmetric matrix with strictly positive eigenvalues is positive definite, and every positive definite matrix is invertible.

This is the fundamental guarantee of ridge regression: for any \(\lambda > 0\), the ridge estimator exists and is unique, regardless of how collinear the features are. Even if \(p > n\) (more features than observations), where OLS is completely undefined, ridge regression still produces a well-defined answer.

Geometric interpretation: shrinking toward zero

What does the penalty actually do to the coefficients? Consider the spectral decomposition of \(X^\top X\). Let its eigenvalues be \(d_1 \geq d_2 \geq \cdots \geq d_{p+1} \geq 0\) with corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_{p+1}\). The OLS estimator can be written in this basis as:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}} = \sum_{j=1}^{p+1} \frac{\mathbf{v}_j^\top X^\top \mathbf{y}}{d_j} \mathbf{v}_j\]

The ridge estimator replaces each \(d_j\) in the denominator with \(d_j + \lambda\):

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} = \sum_{j=1}^{p+1} \frac{\mathbf{v}_j^\top X^\top \mathbf{y}}{d_j + \lambda} \mathbf{v}_j = \sum_{j=1}^{p+1} \frac{d_j}{d_j + \lambda} \cdot \frac{\mathbf{v}_j^\top X^\top \mathbf{y}}{d_j} \mathbf{v}_j\]

Each component of the OLS solution along eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_j\) is multiplied by the shrinkage factor \(\frac{d_j}{d_j + \lambda}\). When \(d_j\) is large (a well-determined direction in feature space), this factor is close to 1 and ridge barely changes the OLS coefficient. When \(d_j\) is small (a poorly determined direction, corresponding to near-collinearity), this factor is close to 0 and ridge shrinks that component aggressively toward zero.

This is exactly the right behavior: directions where the data provides strong signal are preserved; directions where the data is ambiguous are suppressed. The penalty acts as a continuous, adaptive filter on the coefficient space.

Worked example

Ridge vs. OLS under near-collinearity

Let's see the shrinkage in action on a tiny dataset where two features are nearly identical. Suppose \(n = 3\), \(p = 2\), with centered data (so no intercept):

  • \(x_1\): 1, 0, −1
  • \(x_2\): 1.01, 0, −0.99
  • \(y\): 3, 0, −3

Note that \(x_2 \approx x_1\); they differ by at most 0.01. Both are already centered (mean ≈ 0).

Step 1: Build \(X\) and compute \(X^\top X\).

\[X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1.01 \\ 0 & 0 \\ -1 & -0.99 \end{pmatrix}\] \[X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 1^2 + 0^2 + (-1)^2 & 1(1.01) + 0 + (-1)(-0.99) \\ 1.01(1) + 0 + (-0.99)(-1) & 1.01^2 + 0^2 + (-0.99)^2 \end{pmatrix}\]

Computing each entry:

  • \((1,1)\): \(1 + 0 + 1 = 2\)
  • \((1,2)\): \(1.01 + 0.99 = 2.00\)
  • \((2,1)\): \(2.00\) (symmetric)
  • \((2,2)\): \(1.0201 + 0 + 0.9801 = 2.0002\)
\[X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2.0002 \end{pmatrix}\]

The determinant is \(2(2.0002) - 2(2) = 4.0004 - 4 = 0.0004\). This is extremely small — the matrix is nearly singular, confirming severe multicollinearity.

Step 2: Compute \(X^\top \mathbf{y}\).

\[X^\top \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 1(3) + 0(0) + (-1)(-3) \\ 1.01(3) + 0(0) + (-0.99)(-3) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 + 3 \\ 3.03 + 2.97 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\]

Step 3: Compute OLS (for comparison).

Inverting \(X^\top X\):

\[(X^\top X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.0004} \begin{pmatrix} 2.0002 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5000.5 & -5000 \\ -5000 & 5000 \end{pmatrix}\] \[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{OLS}} = \begin{pmatrix} 5000.5 & -5000 \\ -5000 & 5000 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 30003 - 30000 \\ -30000 + 30000 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\]

Wait — these look reasonable. But notice the extreme sensitivity: the coefficients involve differences of numbers around 30,000 to produce answers around 3. If \(y\) changed by just 0.001, the coefficients could shift by hundreds. This is the instability.

Step 4: Compute ridge with \(\lambda = 1\).

\[X^\top X + \lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2.0002 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3.0002 \end{pmatrix}\]

Determinant: \(3(3.0002) - 2(2) = 9.0006 - 4 = 5.0006\). Compare to OLS's 0.0004 — the ridge matrix is enormously better conditioned.

Inverse:

\[(X^\top X + I)^{-1} = \frac{1}{5.0006} \begin{pmatrix} 3.0002 & -2 \\ -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.6000 & -0.3999 \\ -0.3999 & 0.5999 \end{pmatrix}\]

Ridge estimate:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} \approx \begin{pmatrix} 0.6000 & -0.3999 \\ -0.3999 & 0.5999 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3.6000 - 2.3994 \\ -2.3994 + 3.5994 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.2006 \\ 1.2000 \end{pmatrix}\]

Step 5: Interpret the result.

OLS assigned all the predictive weight to \(x_1\) (\(\hat{\beta}_1 = 3, \hat{\beta}_2 = 0\)), an arbitrary choice between two nearly identical features. Ridge splits the weight roughly equally: \(\hat{\beta}_1 \approx 1.20, \hat{\beta}_2 \approx 1.20\). The total predictive effect (\(\approx 2.4\) per unit of either feature) is smaller than OLS's 3, reflecting the shrinkage. But the coefficients are stable, interpretable, and reflect the symmetry of the problem. If we perturbed \(y\) slightly, the ridge coefficients would change smoothly, while the OLS coefficients could swing wildly.

Choosing \(\lambda\): the bias-variance tradeoff

The tuning parameter \(\lambda\) controls a fundamental tradeoff. When \(\lambda = 0\), ridge reduces to OLS: unbiased but potentially high variance under multicollinearity. As \(\lambda \to \infty\), all coefficients shrink to zero: the model predicts \(\bar{y}\) for everything, with zero variance but maximum bias.

The optimal \(\lambda\) balances these extremes. In practice, \(\lambda\) is chosen by cross-validation (covered in Chapter 10): fit ridge at many candidate \(\lambda\) values, evaluate prediction error on held-out folds, and select the \(\lambda\) that minimizes estimated test error. There is no closed-form formula for the optimal \(\lambda\); it depends on the unknown signal-to-noise ratio and the degree of collinearity in the data.

Common mistake

Common mistake: Students sometimes apply the ridge penalty to the intercept \(\beta_0\). This is incorrect because it makes the solution dependent on the arbitrary vertical shift of \(y\). If you add 100 to every \(y_i\), the true relationship hasn't changed, but a penalized intercept would be pulled toward zero differently, producing different slope estimates. Always center \(\mathbf{y}\) and the columns of \(X\) first (making \(\hat{\beta}_0 = 0\)), apply ridge to the slopes only, then add back \(\bar{y}\) as the intercept if needed. Most software libraries handle this automatically, but understanding why matters for interpreting results correctly.

Edge case

What happens as \(\lambda \to 0^+\)? The ridge estimator converges to the OLS estimator whenever \(X^\top X\) is invertible. When \(X^\top X\) is singular, the ridge estimator converges to the minimum-norm OLS solution — the one among the infinitely many OLS solutions that has the smallest \(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\). This is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse solution. So ridge provides a continuous, well-defined extension of OLS into the singular regime, selecting the most parsimonious solution when multiple solutions exist.

What happens as \(\lambda \to \infty\)? Every shrinkage factor \(\frac{d_j}{d_j + \lambda} \to 0\), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{ridge}} \to \mathbf{0}\). The model collapses to predicting the mean of \(y\) (or zero, if centered) for every input. This is the maximum-bias, minimum-variance extreme. No finite dataset requires \(\lambda = \infty\), but very large \(\lambda\) values effectively discard all features.

Where we go next

Ridge regression keeps all features and shrinks their coefficients continuously. But sometimes we want a model that selects a sparse subset of features — setting irrelevant coefficients exactly to zero. Ridge never achieves this because the \(\ell_2\) penalty shrinks smoothly without ever reaching exactly zero. The \(\ell_1\) penalty (Lasso regression) does achieve sparsity, but its non-differentiability requires different optimization techniques. While Lasso is not explicitly on the GATE DA syllabus, understanding ridge deeply prepares you for the bias-variance framework in Chapter 10 and the neural network regularization concepts in Chapter 11, where weight decay is precisely ridge regression applied to network parameters.
