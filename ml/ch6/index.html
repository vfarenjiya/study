6. k-Nearest Neighbour

Chapter 6 of 17 22 min read

The algorithm: learning by analogy

k-nearest neighbour (kNN) is the simplest supervised learning algorithm and the most direct instantiation of the Bayes-optimal predictor introduced in Chapter 1. It has no training phase: the entire dataset is the model. To predict at a query point \(\mathbf{x}_q\), find the \(k\) training points closest to \(\mathbf{x}_q\) under some distance metric, then aggregate their labels:

  • Classification: majority vote among the \(k\) neighbours' labels. Ties are broken randomly, by nearest-neighbour priority, or by weighted voting.

  • Regression: average of the \(k\) neighbours' responses: \(\hat y(\mathbf{x}_q) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_k(\mathbf{x}_q)} y_i\).

kNN is nonparametric: it makes no assumption about the functional form of \(f^*(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = \mathbf{x}]\). As \(n \to \infty\) and \(k \to \infty\) with \(k/n \to 0\), the kNN estimator converges to the true conditional expectation (for regression) or the Bayes classifier (for classification). This universal consistency is its theoretical justification. But finite-sample performance depends critically on three choices: the distance metric, the value of \(k\), and the feature scaling — each of which we analyze in detail below.

Common mistake: treating kNN as "parameter-free." While it has no model parameters like \(\boldsymbol\beta\) in linear regression, \(k\) and the distance metric are hyperparameters that must be tuned via cross-validation (Chapter 12). Choosing \(k = 1\) because it seems natural often leads to severe overfitting; choosing Euclidean distance without standardizing features lets high-variance predictors dominate. These are not implementation details — they are the entire substance of applying kNN well.

Distance metrics and feature scaling

The notion of "nearest" depends entirely on the chosen distance metric. The most common is Euclidean distance (\(\ell_2\)):

\[ d_2(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p} (x_j - x_j')^2} \]

Other choices include Manhattan distance (\(\ell_1\)): \(d_1(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sum_j |x_j - x_j'|\), which is more robust to outliers and performs better in high dimensions; Minkowski distance (\(\ell_p\)): \((\sum_j |x_j - x_j'|^p)^{1/p}\), generalizing both; and domain-specific metrics like cosine similarity for text or Mahalanobis distance when features are correlated.

Critical prerequisite: features must be on comparable scales. If \(x_1\) ranges over \([0, 1]\) and \(x_2\) over \([0, 1000]\), Euclidean distance is dominated entirely by \(x_2\), rendering \(x_1\) irrelevant regardless of its true predictive power. Standard practice is to standardize each feature to zero mean and unit variance before computing distances, or to use min-max scaling to \([0,1]\). When features have different units or semantic meanings, consider whether Euclidean distance is even appropriate — sometimes a domain-informed metric outperforms any generic choice.

Edge case: in very high dimensions (\(p \gg 10\)), all pairwise distances tend to concentrate around a single value, making "nearest" nearly meaningless. For points drawn uniformly from \([0,1]^p\), the ratio \(\max d / \min d \to 1\) as \(p \to \infty\). This is the curse of dimensionality, discussed in detail below. Practical remedies include dimensionality reduction (PCA, Chapter 17), feature selection, or switching to \(\ell_1\) distance, which concentrates less severely than \(\ell_2\). Also note that tied distances are common with discrete or quantized features; deterministic tie-breaking (e.g., by index order) introduces arbitrary bias — randomized or weighted tie-breaking is preferred.

Worked example: distance computation and scale sensitivity

Consider three training points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) with labels: \(A = (1, 100), y_A = 0\); \(B = (2, 200), y_B = 1\); \(C = (3, 150), y_C = 1\). Query point \(\mathbf{x}_q = (2.5, 160)\).

Without scaling: Euclidean distances:

\[ d(A, \mathbf{x}_q) = \sqrt{(2.5-1)^2 + (160-100)^2} = \sqrt{2.25 + 3600} = \sqrt{3602.25} \approx 60.02 \] \[ d(B, \mathbf{x}_q) = \sqrt{(2.5-2)^2 + (160-200)^2} = \sqrt{0.25 + 1600} = \sqrt{1600.25} \approx 40.00 \] \[ d(C, \mathbf{x}_q) = \sqrt{(2.5-3)^2 + (160-150)^2} = \sqrt{0.25 + 100} = \sqrt{100.25} \approx 10.01 \]

For \(k = 2\), nearest neighbours are \(C\) and \(B\) (both label 1) → predict 1. Feature \(x_2\) dominates completely; \(x_1\) contributes negligibly.

With standardization: means \(\bar x_1 = 2\), \(\bar x_2 = 150\); std devs \(s_1 = 0.816\), \(s_2 = 40.82\). Standardized points: \(A' = (-1.225, -1.225)\), \(B' = (0, 1.225)\), \(C' = (1.225, 0)\), \(\mathbf{x}_q' = (0.612, 0.245)\). Distances:

\[ d(A', \mathbf{x}_q') = \sqrt{(0.612+1.225)^2 + (0.245+1.225)^2} = \sqrt{3.376 + 2.161} = \sqrt{5.537} \approx 2.353 \] \[ d(B', \mathbf{x}_q') = \sqrt{(0.612)^2 + (0.245-1.225)^2} = \sqrt{0.375 + 0.960} = \sqrt{1.335} \approx 1.155 \] \[ d(C', \mathbf{x}_q') = \sqrt{(0.612-1.225)^2 + (0.245)^2} = \sqrt{0.376 + 0.060} = \sqrt{0.436} \approx 0.660 \]

Ranking unchanged here (\(C, B, A\)), but the relative gaps shifted dramatically: unscaled, \(d(C)/d(B) \approx 0.25\); scaled, \(d(C)/d(B) \approx 0.57\). In other datasets, scaling can flip the neighbour ranking entirely. Sensitivity check: if \(y_A = 1\) instead of 0, unscaled 2-NN still predicts 1 (neighbours \(C,B\)); scaled 2-NN also predicts 1 (neighbours \(C,B\)). But if \(y_B = 0\), unscaled 2-NN predicts 0 (neighbours \(C,A\) since \(d(A) \lt d(B)\)? No — \(d(A) = 60 > d(B) = 40\), so neighbours remain \(C,B\) → predict 0. Scaled: same neighbours → predict 0. The prediction is robust here, but the margin (distance gap between included and excluded neighbours) differs, affecting confidence estimates.

Bias-variance trade-off in k

The choice of \(k\) controls the fundamental bias-variance trade-off (previewed in Chapter 1, formalized in Chapter 11). Small \(k\) yields low bias but high variance; large \(k\) yields high bias but low variance.

Small \(k\) (e.g., \(k = 1\)): The prediction at \(\mathbf{x}_q\) equals the label of the single nearest neighbour. Bias is minimal (the nearest neighbour's label is an unbiased estimate of \(P(Y|X)\) at a nearby point), but variance is maximal: moving \(\mathbf{x}_q\) slightly can change which point is nearest, causing the prediction to jump discontinuously. The decision boundary is jagged and passes through every training point, memorizing noise.

Large \(k\) (e.g., \(k = n\)): The prediction is the global mean \(\bar y\) everywhere. Variance is zero (prediction doesn't depend on local data), but bias is maximal unless the true function is constant. The decision boundary disappears entirely.

Optimal \(k\): Balances these forces. Theory suggests \(k \propto n^{2/(2+d)}\) for \(d\)-dimensional data under smoothness assumptions, but in practice \(k\) is selected by cross-validation (Chapter 12). Odd \(k\) is preferred for binary classification to avoid ties. Weighted kNN (weighting votes by inverse distance) smooths the transition between \(k\) values and reduces sensitivity to the exact choice.

Common mistake: selecting \(k\) based on training error. Training error monotonically decreases with \(k\) (1-NN achieves zero training error trivially), so it provides no signal about generalization. Always use held-out validation or cross-validation. Also beware that the optimal \(k\) depends on sample size: what works for \(n = 100\) may overfit at \(n = 10000\) or underfit at \(n = 50\). Re-tune \(k\) whenever \(n\) changes substantially.

Worked example: effect of k on predictions

Training data: five points on the real line with labels: \((1, 0), (2, 0), (3, 1), (4, 1), (5, 0)\). Query \(\mathbf{x}_q = 2.8\). Distances to query: \(|2.8-1|=1.8\), \(|2.8-2|=0.8\), \(|2.8-3|=0.2\), \(|2.8-4|=1.2\), \(|2.8-5|=2.2\). Sorted neighbours: \(x=3\) (dist 0.2, label 1), \(x=2\) (0.8, label 0), \(x=4\) (1.2, label 1), \(x=1\) (1.8, label 0), \(x=5\) (2.2, label 0).

Predictions for different \(k\):

\[ \begin{array}{c|l|c} k & \text{Neighbours (labels)} & \text{Prediction} \\ \hline 1 & \{3: 1\} & 1 \\ 2 & \{3: 1, 2: 0\} & \text{tie} \to \text{break to } 1 \text{ (closer)} \\ 3 & \{3: 1, 2: 0, 4: 1\} & 1 \text{ (majority)} \\ 4 & \{3: 1, 2: 0, 4: 1, 1: 0\} & \text{tie} \to \text{break to } 0 \text{ or } 1 \\ 5 & \{3: 1, 2: 0, 4: 1, 1: 0, 5: 0\} & 0 \text{ (majority)} \end{array} \]

The prediction flips from 1 to 0 between \(k = 3\) and \(k = 5\). At \(k = 3\), the local neighborhood captures the positive region around \(x = 3\); at \(k = 5\), the global majority (three zeros) dominates. Sensitivity check: if the true boundary were at \(x = 2.5\) (so \(x = 3\) is genuinely positive), \(k = 3\) is correct and \(k = 5\) is biased. If the true boundary were at \(x = 3.5\) (so \(x = 3\) is actually negative due to label noise), \(k = 1\) and \(k = 3\) are wrong while \(k = 5\) is right. Without knowing the truth, only cross-validation can distinguish these cases — illustrating why \(k\) selection is essential and cannot be done by inspection.

The curse of dimensionality

kNN's performance degrades rapidly as the number of features \(p\) increases, a phenomenon known as the curse of dimensionality. The core issue is geometric: in high dimensions, volume grows exponentially, and data become sparse relative to the space they occupy. Concretely, for \(n\) points uniformly distributed in \([0,1]^p\), the expected distance to the nearest neighbour scales as \(n^{-1/p}\). To maintain the same nearest-neighbour distance when doubling \(p\), you need to square \(n\). At \(p = 20\), achieving the same local density as \(p = 2\) with \(n = 100\) requires \(n \approx 100^{10} = 10^{20}\) points — utterly infeasible.

Beyond sparsity, distance concentration renders neighbourhoods uninformative. For i.i.d. points in \([0,1]^p\), the ratio of maximum to minimum pairwise distances converges to 1 as \(p \to \infty\):

\[ \frac{\max_{i,j} d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)}{\min_{i \neq j} d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)} \xrightarrow{p \to \infty} 1 \]

When all distances are nearly equal, "nearest" loses discriminative power and kNN degenerates toward random guessing. This affects \(\ell_2\) distance most severely; \(\ell_1\) concentrates more slowly, and fractional \(\ell_p\) norms (\(p \lt 1\)) can sometimes help, though they violate the triangle inequality.

Edge case: the curse assumes features are roughly equally informative. When only a few features carry signal and the rest are noise, the effective dimensionality is much lower than \(p\). Feature selection or PCA (Chapter 17) can recover performance by removing noise dimensions. Also note that structured data (images, text) often lie on low-dimensional manifolds embedded in high-dimensional space; kNN can work well in such cases because intrinsic dimensionality is low even if ambient dimensionality is high. Manifold learning methods exploit this explicitly.

Computational considerations

Naive kNN requires computing distances from the query to all \(n\) training points, costing \(O(np)\) per prediction. For large \(n\), this is prohibitive at test time. Two families of acceleration exist:

  • Exact methods: KD-trees and ball trees partition space hierarchically, enabling \(O(p \log n)\) average-case queries in low dimensions (\(p \lt 20\)). Performance degrades to \(O(np)\) in high dimensions due to the curse.

  • Approximate methods: Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH), approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) libraries (FAISS, Annoy, HNSW) sacrifice exactness for speed, returning points that are near-nearest with high probability. Speedups of 100–1000× are typical at modest recall loss.

kNN also stores the entire training set, requiring \(O(np)\) memory. For streaming or memory-constrained settings, condensation algorithms (e.g., CNN, ENN) select a representative subset that preserves classification boundaries. Editing removes noisy or redundant points; condensing retains only points near decision boundaries.

Common mistake: using naive kNN in production without considering computational cost. A model that takes milliseconds to train (because training is free) but seconds per prediction is unusable for real-time systems. Always benchmark query latency at expected \(n\) and \(p\), and invest in indexing or approximation early. Also note that kNN is inherently sequential-friendly but GPU-unfriendly (irregular memory access patterns); neural networks (Chapters 13–14) often win on hardware despite higher algorithmic complexity because they parallelize efficiently.

Worked example: weighted vs. unweighted kNN

Same five-point dataset as before: \((1,0), (2,0), (3,1), (4,1), (5,0)\). Query \(\mathbf{x}_q = 2.8\), \(k = 3\). Neighbours: \(x=3\) (dist 0.2, label 1), \(x=2\) (dist 0.8, label 0), \(x=4\) (dist 1.2, label 1).

Unweighted: votes: 1 gets 2, 0 gets 1 → predict 1.

Inverse-distance weighted: weights \(w_i = 1/d_i\): \(w_3 = 1/0.2 = 5.0\), \(w_2 = 1/0.8 = 1.25\), \(w_4 = 1/1.2 = 0.833\). Weighted votes: class 1 gets \(5.0 + 0.833 = 5.833\), class 0 gets \(1.25\). Predict 1 with much higher confidence (ratio 5.833/1.25 ≈ 4.67 vs. unweighted 2/1 = 2.0).

Sensitivity check: move query to \(\mathbf{x}_q = 2.6\). Distances: \(x=3\) (0.4), \(x=2\) (0.6), \(x=4\) (1.4). Unweighted 3-NN: same neighbours, same prediction (1). Weighted: \(w_3 = 2.5\), \(w_2 = 1.667\), \(w_4 = 0.714\). Class 1: \(2.5 + 0.714 = 3.214\); class 0: \(1.667\). Still predicts 1, but confidence ratio dropped from 4.67 to 1.93 — reflecting increased proximity to the boundary. Weighted kNN provides a continuous confidence signal that unweighted voting discards, making it preferable when downstream decisions depend on prediction certainty (e.g., threshold tuning for cost-sensitive classification).

Connection to the broader framework

kNN occupies a unique position in the supervised learning landscape. Chapter 1 defined the Bayes-optimal predictor; kNN is the most direct empirical approximation to it, requiring no parametric assumptions. Chapter 5's logistic regression models \(P(Y|X)\) parametrically via log-odds; kNN estimates it nonparametrically via local averaging, trading interpretability for flexibility. Chapter 11's bias-variance decomposition applies directly: \(k\) is the knob that trades bias for variance, exactly analogous to \(\lambda\) in ridge regression (Chapter 4) or tree depth in decision trees (Chapter 10). Chapter 12's cross-validation is the primary tool for selecting \(k\), since training error is useless. Chapter 15's k-means clustering can be viewed as an unsupervised analogue: replacing labelled neighbours with cluster centroids. And Chapter 17's PCA is often applied before kNN to mitigate the curse of dimensionality, connecting supervised and unsupervised learning in practice.

kNN also serves as a diagnostic baseline. If a complex model (SVM, neural network) cannot beat kNN on your task, either the problem is simple enough that kNN suffices, or the complex model is misconfigured. Conversely, if kNN fails badly while parametric models succeed, the curse of dimensionality or inappropriate distance metric is likely the culprit. Master kNN not as a production algorithm (though it remains useful for small, low-dimensional problems) but as the conceptual anchor connecting nonparametric estimation, the bias-variance trade-off, and the geometry of feature spaces.
