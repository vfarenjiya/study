8. Support Vector Machines

Chapter 8 of 14 35 min read

Beyond accuracy: why margin matters

In Chapters 4 and 7, we built classifiers (logistic regression, LDA) that produce linear decision boundaries by optimizing probabilistic objectives. These methods find a separating hyperplane when one exists, but not necessarily the best one. Consider two datasets that are both linearly separable: in one, the classes are far apart with a wide gap; in the other, they barely touch. A classifier that merely achieves zero training error treats both identically, yet intuitively the wide-gap case should yield more robust predictions on new data.

The question Support Vector Machines answer is: among all hyperplanes that correctly classify the training data, which one maximizes our confidence in future predictions, and how do we find it?

The answer is geometric: choose the hyperplane that maximizes the margin — the distance from the decision boundary to the nearest training point on either side. A larger margin means the boundary is as far as possible from any observed example, providing the greatest buffer against noise and sampling variability in future data.

Defining the geometric margin

A hyperplane in \(\mathbb{R}^p\) is defined by \(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x + b = 0\), where \(\boldsymbol{w} \in \mathbb{R}^p\) is the normal vector and \(b \in \mathbb{R}\) is the offset. Points with \(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x + b > 0\) lie on one side; points with \(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x + b < 0\) lie on the other.

The perpendicular distance from any point \(x_0\) to this hyperplane is:

\[\text{dist}(x_0, \text{hyperplane}) = \frac{|\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_0 + b|}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|}\]

This is a standard result from analytic geometry: the numerator measures the signed "height" above the plane along the normal direction, and dividing by \(\|\boldsymbol{w}\|\) converts from the arbitrary scale of \(\boldsymbol{w}\) to true Euclidean distance.

For binary classification with labels \(y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\), correct classification means \(y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b) > 0\) for all \(i\). The geometric margin of the classifier \((\boldsymbol{w}, b)\) on the training set is the minimum distance across all training points:

\[\gamma = \min_{i} \frac{y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b)}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|}\]

We want to maximize \(\gamma\) subject to correct classification of all points. But there's a scaling ambiguity: multiplying \(\boldsymbol{w}\) and \(b\) by any positive constant \(c\) doesn't change the hyperplane or the classification, but it scales both the numerator and denominator of \(\gamma\) by \(c\), leaving \(\gamma\) unchanged. To remove this ambiguity, we impose a canonical scaling constraint.

The hard-margin SVM primal problem

We fix the scale by requiring that the closest points satisfy \(y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b) \geq 1\). Under this constraint, the geometric margin becomes exactly \(\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|}\). Maximizing \(\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|}\) is equivalent to minimizing \(\|\boldsymbol{w}\|\), which is equivalent to minimizing \(\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2\) (the factor of \(\frac{1}{2}\) simplifies derivatives later without changing the optimum).

This gives the hard-margin SVM primal problem:

\[\boxed{\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b} \frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b) \geq 1 \;\; \forall i = 1,\ldots,n}This is a convex quadratic program with linear inequality constraints. It has a unique global minimum (since \(\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2\) is strictly convex) whenever the data is linearly separable. If the data is not separable, the constraints are infeasible and no solution exists — we address this with soft-margin SVM below.

Deriving the dual via Lagrange multipliers

Solving the primal directly is possible but obscures the structure of the solution. The dual formulation reveals that only a subset of training points (the support vectors) determine the optimal hyperplane, and it enables the kernel trick for nonlinear classification.

We form the Lagrangian with multipliers \(\alpha_i \geq 0\) for each constraint:

\[\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \big[y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b) - 1\big]\]

To obtain the dual, we minimize \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(\boldsymbol{w}\) and \(b\) (the primal variables), then maximize over \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) (the dual variables).

Step 1: Minimize w.r.t. \(\boldsymbol{w}\)

Taking the gradient of \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(\boldsymbol{w}\) and setting to zero:

\[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{w}} \mathcal{L} = \boldsymbol{w} - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i x_i = \mathbf{0}\] \[\boldsymbol{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i x_i\]

This tells us the optimal weight vector is a linear combination of the training points, weighted by their Lagrange multipliers and labels.

Step 2: Minimize w.r.t. \(b\)

Taking the derivative of \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to \(b\) and setting to zero:

\[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i = 0\] \[\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i = 0\]

This is a constraint on the dual variables: the weighted sum of labels must be zero.

Step 3: Substitute back into the Lagrangian

Substituting \(\boldsymbol{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\) into \(\mathcal{L}\):

\[\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i x_i\right\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_j y_j x_j\right)^\top x_i - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i b + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i\]

The \(b\) term vanishes because \(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0\). Expanding the squared norm as a double sum and simplifying:

\[\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j - \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i\] \[= -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i\]

The dual problem is to maximize this expression (equivalently, minimize its negation) subject to \(\alpha_i \geq 0\) and \(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0\):

\[\boxed{\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j x_i^\top x_j \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \alpha_i \geq 0, \;\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i = 0}Critically, the dual depends on the data only through the inner products \(x_i^\top x_j\). This is what enables the kernel trick.

Support vectors and sparsity

The KKT complementary slackness condition states that at optimality:

\[\alpha_i \big[y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b) - 1\big] = 0 \quad \forall i\]

This means for every training point, either \(\alpha_i = 0\) or \(y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b) = 1\) (or both). Points with \(\alpha_i > 0\) lie exactly on the margin boundary — these are the support vectors. Points with \(\alpha_i = 0\) are irrelevant to the solution: removing them from the training set would not change the optimal hyperplane.

Since \(\boldsymbol{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\), only support vectors contribute to \(\boldsymbol{w}\). Similarly, prediction for a new point \(x^*\) requires only:

\[f(x^*) = \boldsymbol{w}^\top x^* + b = \sum_{i: \alpha_i > 0} \alpha_i y_i x_i^\top x^* + b\]

The solution is sparse: typically only a small fraction of training points are support vectors, making prediction efficient even when \(n\) is large.

Worked example

Hard-margin SVM on four points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)

Consider four training points with labels \(y \in \{-1, +1\}\):

  • \(x_1 = (1, 1)\), \(y_1 = +1\)
  • \(x_2 = (2, 2)\), \(y_2 = +1\)
  • \(x_3 = (1, 0)\), \(y_3 = -1\)
  • \(x_4 = (2, -1)\), \(y_4 = -1\)

Step 1: Visualize and identify candidate support vectors.

The positive points are at \((1,1)\) and \((2,2)\); the negative points are at \((1,0)\) and \((2,-1)\). Intuitively, the closest pair across classes is \(x_1 = (1,1)\) and \(x_3 = (1,0)\), separated vertically by distance 1. Points \(x_2\) and \(x_4\) are farther from the boundary and likely not support vectors.

Step 2: Solve assuming only \(x_1\) and \(x_3\) are support vectors.

If only \(\alpha_1, \alpha_3 > 0\) and \(\alpha_2 = \alpha_4 = 0\), the constraint \(\sum \alpha_i y_i = 0\) gives:

\[\alpha_1(+1) + \alpha_3(-1) = 0 \implies \alpha_1 = \alpha_3\]

Let \(\alpha_1 = \alpha_3 = \alpha\). The dual objective becomes:

\[D(\alpha) = 2\alpha - \frac{1}{2}\big[\alpha^2 y_1^2 x_1^\top x_1 + 2\alpha^2 y_1 y_3 x_1^\top x_3 + \alpha^2 y_3^2 x_3^\top x_3\big]\]

Computing inner products:

  • \(x_1^\top x_1 = 1 + 1 = 2\)
  • \(x_3^\top x_3 = 1 + 0 = 1\)
  • \(x_1^\top x_3 = 1 + 0 = 1\)
  • \(y_1 y_3 = (+1)(-1) = -1\)
\[D(\alpha) = 2\alpha - \frac{1}{2}\big[2\alpha^2 + 2(-1)\alpha^2(1) + \alpha^2\big] = 2\alpha - \frac{1}{2}(2\alpha^2 - 2\alpha^2 + \alpha^2) = 2\alpha - \frac{\alpha^2}{2}\]

Maximizing: \(\frac{dD}{d\alpha} = 2 - \alpha = 0 \implies \alpha = 2\).

So \(\alpha_1 = \alpha_3 = 2\), \(\alpha_2 = \alpha_4 = 0\).

Step 3: Recover \(\boldsymbol{w}\) and \(b\).

\[\boldsymbol{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i = 2(+1)\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} + 2(-1)\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}2\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\2\end{pmatrix}\]

To find \(b\), use any support vector. For \(x_1\): \(y_1(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_1 + b) = 1\):

\[(+1)\left(\begin{pmatrix}0 & 2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} + b\right) = 1 \implies 2 + b = 1 \implies b = -1\]

Verify with \(x_3\): \((-1)(0 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 0 - 1) = (-1)(-1) = 1\). ✓

Step 4: State the classifier and margin.

The decision boundary is \(0 \cdot x_1 + 2 \cdot x_2 - 1 = 0\), i.e., \(x_2 = 0.5\). The geometric margin is:

\[\gamma = \frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0^2 + 2^2}} = \frac{1}{2}\]

Step 5: Verify non-support vectors satisfy constraints.

  • \(x_2 = (2,2)\): \(y_2(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_2 + b) = (+1)(4 - 1) = 3 \geq 1\). ✓ (\(\alpha_2 = 0\))
  • \(x_4 = (2,-1)\): \(y_4(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_4 + b) = (-1)(-2 - 1) = 3 \geq 1\). ✓ (\(\alpha_4 = 0\))

All KKT conditions are satisfied. The optimal hyperplane is horizontal at \(x_2 = 0.5\), with margin \(0.5\), determined entirely by the two support vectors \(x_1\) and \(x_3\).

Soft-margin SVM: handling non-separable data

Real data is rarely perfectly separable. The hard-margin SVM fails entirely when even one point violates the margin constraint. The soft-margin SVM introduces slack variables \(\xi_i \geq 0\) that allow violations at a cost:

\[\min_{\boldsymbol{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\xi}} \frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^{n} \xi_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b) \geq 1 - \xi_i, \;\; \xi_i \geq 0\]

The parameter \(C > 0\) controls the tradeoff: large \(C\) penalizes misclassifications heavily (approaching hard-margin); small \(C\) prioritizes a wider margin even at the cost of some training errors. The dual is identical to the hard-margin dual except with the additional constraint \(\alpha_i \leq C\). Points with \(0 < \alpha_i < C\) are support vectors on the margin; points with \(\alpha_i = C\) are either on the wrong side of the margin or misclassified.

The kernel trick

The dual SVM depends on data only through inner products \(x_i^\top x_j\) and \(x_i^\top x^*\). If we replace every inner product with a function \(K(x_i, x_j)\) that computes an inner product in some higher-dimensional feature space \(\phi(x)\) without explicitly computing \(\phi\), we get a nonlinear classifier in the original space while retaining the computational efficiency of the dual.

Common kernels include the polynomial kernel \(K(x, x') = (x^\top x' + c)^d\) and the RBF (Gaussian) kernel \(K(x, x') = \exp(-\gamma\|x - x'\|^2)\). Mercer's theorem guarantees that any symmetric positive semidefinite function corresponds to some implicit feature map \(\phi\), so we never need to specify \(\phi\) explicitly.

Common mistake

Common mistake: Students often confuse the functional margin \(y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b)\) with the geometric margin \(\frac{y_i(\boldsymbol{w}^\top x_i + b)}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|}\). The functional margin depends on the arbitrary scaling of \(\boldsymbol{w}\) and \(b\); doubling \(\boldsymbol{w}\) doubles the functional margin without changing the actual geometry. Only the geometric margin is scale-invariant and meaningful as a measure of separation quality. The SVM optimization fixes the functional margin to 1 via the canonical scaling constraint precisely to make the geometric margin equal to \(1/\|\boldsymbol{w}\|\), converting margin maximization into norm minimization.

Edge case

What happens as \(C \to \infty\) in soft-margin SVM? The penalty for any \(\xi_i > 0\) becomes infinite, forcing all \(\xi_i = 0\). The soft-margin problem reduces exactly to the hard-margin problem. If the data is not linearly separable, the problem becomes infeasible. Conversely, as \(C \to 0\), the margin term dominates and the optimizer drives \(\|\boldsymbol{w}\| \to 0\), collapsing the decision boundary toward the prior mean with maximum margin but ignoring the data entirely. The optimal \(C\) is always chosen by cross-validation (Chapter 10).

What if all points are support vectors? This occurs when every training point lies exactly on the margin boundary, which happens in degenerate configurations (e.g., two parallel lines of points with no interior points). The solution is still valid but loses sparsity: prediction requires all \(n\) terms. This is rare in practice but highlights that sparsity is a typical property, not a guaranteed one.

Where we go next

SVMs find optimal linear (or kernelized) boundaries but provide no probabilistic output and require solving a quadratic program. Chapter 9 introduces Decision Trees, which partition feature space via axis-aligned splits, producing interpretable, nonparametric classifiers that handle mixed feature types naturally and require no optimization at all — just recursive greedy splitting. Decision trees also form the building blocks for ensemble methods like random forests and gradient boosting, which dominate practical machine learning competitions.
