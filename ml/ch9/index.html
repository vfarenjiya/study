9. Support Vector Machine

Chapter 9 of 17 26 min read

The maximum-margin principle

The Support Vector Machine (SVM) approaches binary classification from a geometric perspective fundamentally different from the probabilistic models of Chapters 5, 7, and 8. Rather than modeling distributions or minimizing average loss, SVM seeks the hyperplane that maximizes the margin — the distance to the nearest training point on either side. This principle is motivated by statistical learning theory: among all separating hyperplanes, the one with largest margin has the tightest generalization bound, independent of the ambient dimensionality \(p\).

For linearly separable data with labels \(y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\), a hyperplane is defined by \(\{\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0\}\). The signed distance from point \(\mathbf{x}_i\) to this hyperplane is \((\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) / \|\mathbf{w}\|\). Correct classification requires \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) > 0\). The geometric margin is the minimum such distance across all training points:

\[ \gamma = \min_i \frac{y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b)}{\|\mathbf{w}\|} \]

We can rescale \(\mathbf{w}, b\) without changing the hyperplane so that the closest points satisfy \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1\). Under this canonical scaling, the margin becomes \(1/\|\mathbf{w}\|\), and maximizing it is equivalent to minimizing \(\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\). This yields the hard-margin SVM primal problem:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{w}, b} \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1, \; i = 1, \dots, n \]

This is a convex quadratic program with linear constraints. Its solution defines the unique maximum-margin separator when data are separable.

Common mistake: confusing functional margin \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b)\) with geometric margin \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b)/\|\mathbf{w}\|\). Functional margin depends on the arbitrary scaling of \(\mathbf{w}\); geometric margin does not. The constraint \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1\) fixes the functional margin at 1 for support vectors, which implicitly sets the geometric margin to \(1/\|\mathbf{w}\|\). Always reason about generalization in terms of geometric margin; functional margin alone is meaningless without normalization.

The dual formulation and support vectors

Solving the primal directly is possible but obscures the structure that makes SVMs powerful. The Lagrangian introduces multipliers \(\alpha_i \geq 0\) for each constraint:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol\alpha) = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \big[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\big] \]

Setting derivatives w.r.t. \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\) to zero gives stationarity conditions:

\[ \mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i y_i = 0 \]

Substituting back eliminates \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\), yielding the dual problem:

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol\alpha} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \alpha_i \geq 0, \; \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0 \]

Critically, the data appear only through inner products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\). By complementary slackness, \(\alpha_i > 0\) only for points satisfying \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1\) — these are the support vectors, the training points lying exactly on the margin boundary. All other points have \(\alpha_i = 0\) and do not influence the solution. The optimal hyperplane depends only on this typically small subset, giving SVM its name and its computational efficiency at prediction time.

Edge case: when data are not linearly separable, the hard-margin primal is infeasible (no \(\mathbf{w}, b\) satisfy all constraints). The dual would be unbounded. Soft-margin SVM (next section) resolves this by allowing violations. Also note that support vectors are not unique when multiple points lie exactly on the margin; the solution \(\mathbf{w}\) is unique, but the set of active \(\alpha_i\) may not be. Degenerate cases (e.g., duplicate points on opposite margins) require careful implementation to avoid numerical instability.

Worked example: hand-computed hard-margin SVM

Consider four points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\): class +1 at \((2,3)\) and \((3,3)\); class −1 at \((1,1)\) and \((2,1)\). These are linearly separable. By inspection, the optimal separating line is horizontal between \(y=1\) and \(y=3\), i.e., \(w_2 y + b = 0\) with \(w_1 = 0\). Let’s derive it formally.

Step 1: Identify candidate support vectors. The closest cross-class pairs are \((2,1)\)↔\((2,3)\) and \((1,1)\)↔\((2,3)\) etc. Vertical gap between \(y=1\) and \(y=3\) is 2; margin = 1. Guess support vectors: \((2,3)\) (+1) and \((2,1)\) (−1). Check if others satisfy margin ≥ 1 under resulting hyperplane.

Step 2: Solve for \(\mathbf{w}, b\) using support vector conditions. For SVs, \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1\):

\[ +(w_1 \cdot 2 + w_2 \cdot 3 + b) = 1, \qquad -(w_1 \cdot 2 + w_2 \cdot 1 + b) = 1 \]

Adding: \(2w_2 = 2 \Rightarrow w_2 = 1\). Subtracting second from first: \(2w_2 + 2b = 0 \Rightarrow 2 + 2b = 0 \Rightarrow b = -1\). From symmetry, expect \(w_1 = 0\); verify: plug into first equation: \(0 + 3 - 1 = 2 \neq 1\). Contradiction! Our guess was wrong — \((2,3)\) and \((2,1)\) alone don’t determine the max-margin plane because \((3,3)\) might be closer to some tilted boundary.

Step 3: Re-examine geometry. Midpoint between classes vertically is \(y=2\). Horizontal line \(y=2\) gives margin 1 to both \((2,3)\) and \((2,1)\), and also to \((3,3)\) (distance 1) and \((1,1)\) (distance 1). So ALL FOUR points are support vectors! Margin = 1, \(\|\mathbf{w}\| = 1\) (since margin = \(1/\|\mathbf{w}\|\)). Hyperplane: \(0 \cdot x_1 + 1 \cdot x_2 - 2 = 0\), i.e., \(x_2 = 2\). Verify: \(y_i(w^\top x_i + b) = +1(3-2)=1\), \(+1(3-2)=1\), \(-1(1-2)=1\), \(-1(1-2)=1\). All equal 1 ✓. \(\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 = 1\), objective = 0.5.

Sensitivity check: move \((3,3)\) to \((3,4)\). Now vertical distances: \((3,4)\) is 2 above \(y=2\), \((2,3)\) is 1 above. New optimum tilts slightly to increase margin toward \((3,4)\) at expense of \((2,3)\)? Actually no — \((2,3)\) and \((2,1)/(1,1)\) still constrain margin to ≤1. Moving \((3,3)\) outward doesn’t change the bottleneck; support vectors remain the three original closest points. But if we moved \((2,3)\) to \((2,4)\), then \((3,3)\) becomes the new bottleneck SV, and the boundary shifts. This illustrates that only the closest points matter — adding distant points never changes the SVM solution, a key robustness property.

Soft margin and the regularization parameter C

Real data are rarely perfectly separable. Soft-margin SVM introduces slack variables \(\xi_i \geq 0\) measuring constraint violation:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol\xi} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{n} \xi_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1 - \xi_i, \; \xi_i \geq 0 \]

The parameter \(C > 0\) controls the trade-off: large \(C\) penalizes misclassifications heavily (approaching hard margin); small \(C\) prioritizes wide margins even at the cost of more errors. The dual becomes identical to the hard-margin case except for box constraints \(0 \leq \alpha_i \leq C\). Points with \(\alpha_i = C\) are either misclassified (\(\xi_i > 1\)) or on the wrong side of the margin (\(0 < \xi_i \leq 1\)); points with \(0 < \alpha_i < C\) lie exactly on the margin (\(\xi_i = 0\)); points with \(\alpha_i = 0\) are correctly classified beyond the margin.

This reveals SVM as ERM with hinge loss plus \(\ell_2\) regularization (connecting to Chapter 1 and Chapter 4):

\[ \min_{\mathbf{w}, b} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{n} \max(0, 1 - y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b)) \]

Hinge loss is zero when \(y_i f(\mathbf{x}_i) \geq 1\) (correct with sufficient margin), linear otherwise. Unlike logistic loss (Chapter 5), it provides no gradient signal once a point is safely classified, leading to sparse solutions where only support vectors matter.

Common mistake: interpreting \(C\) as “number of allowed errors.” It is a continuous penalty weight, not a count. Small \(C\) allows many small violations or few large ones depending on data geometry. Also, \(C\) must be tuned via cross-validation (Chapter 12); there is no universal default. A common heuristic is \(C = 1/n\) or grid search over powers of 10, but optimal values vary widely across datasets.

The kernel trick and nonlinear boundaries

The dual depends on data only through inner products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\). The kernel trick replaces these with a function \(K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j)\) that computes an inner product in some (possibly infinite-dimensional) feature space \(\mathcal{F}\) without explicitly mapping \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto \phi(\mathbf{x})\):

\[ K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \langle \phi(\mathbf{x}), \phi(\mathbf{x}') \rangle_{\mathcal{F}} \]

Common kernels include:

  • Polynomial: \(K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = (\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}' + c)^d\) — captures interactions up to degree \(d\).
  • RBF (Gaussian): \(K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp(-\gamma \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2)\) — corresponds to infinite-dimensional \(\mathcal{F}\); most widely used.
  • Sigmoid: \(K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \tanh(\kappa \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}' + c)\) — neural network-inspired; not always positive definite.

Mercer’s theorem guarantees that any symmetric positive semidefinite kernel corresponds to some valid inner product space. The SVM dual with kernel \(K\) finds the maximum-margin separator in \(\mathcal{F}\), which corresponds to a potentially highly nonlinear decision boundary in input space — all while solving the same finite-dimensional QP.

Edge case: RBF kernel with very large \(\gamma\) makes \(K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) \approx 0\) for \(i \neq j\), causing every training point to become a support vector and the decision function to interpolate training labels (overfitting). Very small \(\gamma\) makes \(K \approx 1\) everywhere, collapsing to a constant predictor (underfitting). Both \(\gamma\) and \(C\) must be jointly tuned; typical practice uses log-grid search with cross-validation. Also note that kernel SVM prediction costs \(O(n_{SV} \cdot p)\) per test point since \(K(\mathbf{x}_{test}, \mathbf{x}_i)\) must be evaluated for every support vector — potentially slow for large \(n_{SV}\). Approximation methods (Nyström, random Fourier features) address this.

Worked example: polynomial kernel expansion

Take the four-point dataset from earlier but add a non-separable point: class +1 at \((2,3), (3,3), (1,2)\); class −1 at \((1,1), (2,1)\). Point \((1,2)\) lies between the original classes, breaking linear separability in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Use degree-2 polynomial kernel \(K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = (\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}' + 1)^2\).

The implicit feature map for \(d=2, p=2\) is \(\phi(x_1,x_2) = (x_1^2, x_2^2, \sqrt{2}x_1 x_2, \sqrt{2}x_1, \sqrt{2}x_2, 1)\) (6D). In this space, the previously non-separable configuration may become separable because quadratic terms capture curvature. Without solving the full dual (tedious by hand), note that \(K((1,2),(1,1)) = (1+2+1)^2 = 16\) while \(K((1,2),(2,1)) = (2+2+1)^2 = 25\) — the kernel distinguishes proximity in ways Euclidean distance cannot. The SVM in \(\mathcal{F}\) finds a linear separator corresponding to a conic section (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Sensitivity check: with linear kernel \(K = \mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}'\), soft-margin SVM would place \((1,2)\) as a misclassified support vector with \(\alpha_i = C\); with RBF kernel and appropriate \(\gamma\), it could wrap around \((1,2)\) correctly. Kernel choice fundamentally determines the hypothesis class complexity.

Connection to other classifiers and practical considerations

SVM occupies a distinct niche among supervised learners. Compared to logistic regression (Chapter 5), SVM optimizes margin rather than likelihood, yielding sparser solutions (only SVs matter) but no probabilistic outputs (Platt scaling approximates posteriors post-hoc). Compared to LDA (Chapter 8), SVM makes no distributional assumptions and handles non-separability gracefully via soft margin, but loses interpretability of coefficients. Compared to kNN (Chapter 6), SVM learns a compact parametric boundary rather than storing all data, enabling faster prediction but losing adaptivity to local density. Compared to neural networks (Chapters 13–14), SVM offers stronger theoretical guarantees and easier hyperparameter tuning for small-to-medium datasets, but scales worse to massive data and lacks representation learning.

Practical tips: always standardize features before applying SVM (margin is scale-sensitive); use RBF kernel as default unless domain knowledge suggests otherwise; tune \(C\) and \(\gamma\) jointly via cross-validation; prefer libsvm/liblinear implementations over custom solvers; consider linear SVM (liblinear) for high-dimensional sparse data (text) where kernel SVM is unnecessary and slow. For multiclass, use one-vs-one (preferred) or one-vs-rest strategies.

Common mistake: applying SVM to massive datasets (\(n > 10^5\)) without considering scalability. Standard SVM training is \(O(n^2)\) to \(O(n^3)\); use linear SVM with SGD, approximate kernels, or switch to neural networks. Also beware that SVM’s margin-based theory assumes i.i.d. data; under distribution shift or adversarial perturbation, max-margin solutions can be brittle despite good test accuracy.

Connection to the broader framework

SVM synthesizes several core themes from this book. Chapter 1 introduced hinge loss as a surrogate for 0-1 loss; SVM is ERM with hinge loss plus \(\ell_2\) regularization, directly paralleling ridge regression (Chapter 4) but with a different loss. Chapter 3’s projection geometry reappears in the margin derivation and dual formulation. Chapter 5’s logistic regression shares the linear-in-parameters structure but differs in optimization objective and sparsity properties. Chapter 11’s bias-variance trade-off manifests in \(C\): large \(C\) reduces bias (fits tightly) at cost of variance (sensitive to SVs); small \(C\) increases bias (wider margin) but reduces variance. Chapter 12’s cross-validation is essential for tuning \(C\) and kernel parameters. And Chapter 17’s PCA connects via kernel PCA, which applies the kernel trick to unsupervised dimensionality reduction, showing how SVM’s mathematical machinery transcends classification.

Master SVM not merely as a classifier but as the paradigmatic example of convex optimization in machine learning, duality theory in action, and the power of implicit feature spaces. If you can derive the dual from the primal, identify support vectors geometrically, explain why kernels enable nonlinearity without explicit mapping, and articulate when SVM beats logistic regression (and vice versa), you have internalized the optimization-centric view of learning that complements the probabilistic view of earlier chapters.
